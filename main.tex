\section{Motivation}

\begin{marginfigure}
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{analyses/launch_mass_plot_no_starlink.pdf}

Out of more than \num{23000} satellites that have been launched into Earth orbit \autocite{esaspacedebrisofficeESAsAnnualSpace2025}, more than \num{2900} belong in the \emph{nano-satellite} class \autocite{kuluNanosatsDatabase2023}, weighing less than \SI{10}{\kilogram}.
Nano-satellites are mostly represented by the CubeSat form factor, a concept introduced in 1999 by the \citeauthor{CDS14} \autocite{CDS14}.

CubeSats are designed in a modular format consisting of \qtyproduct{10 x 10 x 10}{\centi\meter} cubes (termed ``U(nits)''). Subsystems typically follow the ``PC/104'' mechanical standard for subsystems, along with a shared electrical specification \autocite{BoardSpecificationLibreCube}.

The advances of miniaturisation in electronics since the 1980s have gradually increased the capabilities and utility of nano-satellites \autocite{sweetingModernSmallSatellitesChanging2018}. Services such as Earth Observation, Telecommunications and Technology Demonstration are very frequently covered by missions comprising one or more CubeSats\footnote{21 constellations of 5 or more nano-satellites have been launched so far. Additionally, CubeSats represent over \qty{90}{\percent} of all nano-satellites launched  \autocite{kuluNanosatsDatabase2023}}. Most CubeSats are orbiting in \acs{LEO}, with a few missions also planned and launched for deep space \autocite{walkerDeepspaceCubeSatsThinking2018}.

The low cost of CubeSat launches (typically \(\sim\)\SI{100000}{USD/\kilogram}) \draft{ref} has significantly reduced the entry barrier to space
\autocite{poghosyanCubeSatEvolutionAnalyzing2017,sweetingModernSmallSatellitesChanging2018}.
\draft{NUM\%} of launched CubeSat missions are educational in nature. \draft{NUM} countries in total have launched CubeSats, \draft{NUM} of which have CubeSats as their first satellite. This has been aided by an evolving CubeSat industry, which has created a market for subsystems and platforms that are, to some degree, interoperable with each other and with common deployers. As of 2023, roughly 20\% of spacecraft in orbit\footnote{Excluding the Starlink constellation, which represents \qty{53}{\percent} \cite{unionofconcernedscientistsSatelliteDatabase2023}} belong in the nano-satellite class \cite{unionofconcernedscientistsSatelliteDatabase2023} (\Cref{fig:i_mass_distribution}), with 25 CubeSats being launched per year on average since 2020 \cite{kuluNanosatsDatabase2023}, and a more than \num{500} million USD market in 2025 \cite{CubeSatMarketSize}.

Despite the significantly fewer resources needed for CubeSat development and production, there is still a significant cost connected with CubeSat missions. Launch and development costs can range up to millions USD \draft{[ref from SW/ppr]}, and development times can range between \draft{X-Y years} \draft{[ref from SW/ppr]}. Despite their reduced complexity, CubeSats also experience a \qty{39}{\percent} failure rate in orbit, often attributed to ad-hoc procedures, insufficient testing to uncover mistakes, and workmanship flaws \autocite{swartwoutCubeSatMissionSuccess2019}.

The need from industry and academia for a smaller and less complex standardised platform has given rise to concepts for so-called \textbf{\acf{SCS}}. These spacecraft take advantage of the further miniaturisation of electronic and telecommunication components to push the envelope of size. Classes of planned or launched \acs{SCS} include:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=-1ex]
	\item \textbf{Fractional-Unit} CubeSats: 1/2U, 1/3U or 1/4U CubeSats, which do not comply with the CubeSat standard \cite{CDS14}, but are mechanically compatible with standard deployers \autocite{kuluNanosatsDatabase2023,krebsThinSat1A1L2021};
	\item \textbf{PocketQubes}: Modular spacecraft based on the ``1p'' unit (a \qtyproduct{5 x 5 x 5}{\centi\meter}) \autocite{PQ1};
	\item \textbf{SunCubes}: Modular spacecraft based on \qtyproduct{3 x 3 x 3}{\centi\meter} units \autocite{martinezSunCubeFemtoSatDesign2018};
	\item \textbf{Chipsats} or \textbf{\acs{PCB}sats}: Flat spacecraft that consist entirely of a single circuit board, usually less than \qtyproduct{5 x 5}{\centi\meter} in size.
\end{itemize}

These satellites are also classified as pico-, femto- and atto-satellites (see \Cref{sec:LITERATURE_classes}).

\acl{SCS} have enjoyed increased popularity especially since 2018, with \draft{NUM} pico-satellites and \draft{NUM} smaller satellites having been launched (\Cref{fig:i_scs_launched}). Most of these \draft{how many?} follow the \textbf{PocketQube} standard.
The PocketQube concept was introduced in 2009 by Bob Twiggs \autocite{twiggsMakingItSmall2009} and standardised in \citeyear{PQ1} \autocite{PQ1}. Thanks to the availability of deployers targetting different sizes of PocketQubes, this form factor has enjoyed an average rate of \num{18} satellites launched per year since 2021 \autocite{wikipediacontributorsListLaunchedPocketQubes2025}.

On the sub-PocketQube scale, one of the most notable cases were Zac Manchester's ``Sprite'' ChipSats. Measuring \qtyproduct{3.5 x 3.5}{\centi\metre} (\Cref{subfig:i_manchester_chipsat}), more than 100 ChipSats were deployed by Manchester in 2019, with the goal of allowing members of the public to have ``their personal spacecraft in space'', and marking the first known occasion where atto-satellites were launched into orbit \autocite{manchesterCentimeterScaleSpacecraftDesign2015,thenationalassociationforamateurradioKickSat2AliveBeing2019}.

The distinct characteristics of PocketQubes, ChipSats and similar spacecraft, \draft{revolving mainly around size and cost}, have been explored in the literature as they motivate capabilities which cannot normally be attained by larger spacecraft \autocite{perezSurveyCurrentFemtosatellite2016,heinAttoSatsChipSatsOther2019}. One notable example is the concept of Breakthrough Starshot, which exploits the small mass of sail-equipped ChipSats to propel them beyond the Solar System using terrestrial lasers \autocite{finkbeinerNearLightSpeedMissionAlpha2017}. Other proposed concepts for \acs{SCS} include distributed data collection, mother-ship based missions, and rapid testing \cite{heinAttoSatsChipSatsOther2019}.

Due to the smaller size and component count of \acs{SCS}, these systems are generally simpler and easier to develop than their larger counterparts. While the capabilities of a single system are reduced due to these constraints, these form factors make spacecraft development accessible to engineers and communities that would otherwise not be able to follow the complete \ac{MAIV} process for a CubeSat, with an extreme example being Manchester's \qty{300}{USD} ChipSat Kickstarter cost \todo{[ref]}. PocketQubes have been described as suitable for education and training in academic environments, especially due to the decreased manufacturing and launch costs \autocite{bouwmeesterSurveyWorldwidePico2010}. \citeauthor{heinAttoSatsChipSatsOther2019} \autocite{heinAttoSatsChipSatsOther2019} also note that the development of flight hardware for ChipSats can be finished within 6 months, costing on the order of dozens to hundreds of dollars for manufacturing, and allowing fast build-and-launch cycles.

However, an investigation of available data for \acs{SCS} missions reveals a different story; even though their complexity and scale is low, their development time follows a median of \draft{26 months}, going up to \draft{62 months} (\Cref{tab:i_otherdevtime}). Yet, outliers that develop systems with similar constraints and form factors at or below a 1 year limit are present. 

This observation is combined with the fact that, in contrast to CubeSats and larger spacecraft, there is \textbf{no widely established \acl{SYE} methodology} associated with PocketQubes, ChipSats, and other \acs{SCS}. As a result, developers need to either adopt existing approaches from larger systems, or develop their own ad-hoc methodologies. 

The combination of small scale with mission criticality is one that is not commonly found in other engineering fields. \acs{SCS} systems can therefore benefit from a tailored methodology that can take advantage of their unique characteristics. Using a \textbf{methodology tailored} for larger spacecraft or systems can introduce significant overhead in terms of cost and time, which may be unnecessary for these systems \todo{[cite?]}. On the other hand, the use of an \textbf{ad-hoc approach} increases programmatic and technical risk, since it provides no guarantees on the timely results of a project and no observability on factors such as progress, quality, or the critical path \autocite{honourTechnicalReportValue2004}. Nevertheless, due to the mission-critical nature of \acs{SCS} and their integration with larger systems,\footnote{Such as launchers, deployers} develpers need to achieve a correct balance between the effort and time spent across design, realisation and verification \autocite{sebok_value,boehmROISystemsEngineering2008,dillonFasterBetterCheaperProjectsToo2015}.

\newthought{Thus,} a \textbf{tailored \acl{SYE} methodology} specifically targetting \acf{SCS} can take advantage of their unique characteristics to reduce time-to-orbit and cost, while maintaining a reasonable level of reliability and \draft{features}. This methodology can also serve as a guideline for new missions, allowing first-time developers to \draft{have} a starting point for new projects, drawing on lessons learned from previous missions. This methodology should cover the aspects of \acl{SYE} that affect high-level planning and design, as well as daily work, being concrete enough to not require ``reinventing the wheel'', but flexible enough to be tailored for different pocket-sized projects. In practical terms, it should cover the entire development lifecycle of a spacecraft, from design and technical management to implementation and validation.

In \Cref{sec:INTRODUCTION_defining_systems_engineering}, I will further analyse the concept of \acl{SYE}, showing how it can be partitioned into different fields, and making \draft{introductory} remarks on its \draft{usefulness} and current approaches. \Cref{sec:INTRODUCTION_research_gaps} will explore the gaps in \acl{SYE} research in the field of \acf{SCS}, and analyse the shortcomings of current approaches. Finally, \Cref{sec:INTRODUCTION_research_questions,sec:INTRODUCTION_structure} will explain how I plan to address these shortcomings, by gathering data to synthesise and verify a bespoke \acs{SYE} methodology for pocket-sized systems.

\section{The Scope and Impact of Systems Engineering}
\label{sec:INTRODUCTION_defining_systems_engineering}

Systems Engineering is defined as a process of synthesis, development and operation of a real-world system that satisfies the requirements for that system \autocite{sebok}.
Systems Engineering processes are especially useful for complex and/or critical systems, such as those used in aerospace engineering \autocite{SMAD,sheaNASASystemsEngineering2020}.
The \acs{NASA} Systems Engineering Handbook partitions Systems Engineering into further specific sub-disciplines, showing its connection with Project Management (\Cref{fig:i_nasa_sye_decomposition}).


%<span class="orange">Can add more criticism towards heavyweight methods here, esp. for systems **why do we need something new?**</span> 

\begin{figure}[h]
	\includegraphics{media/graphics/NASASYE}
	\caption[Decomposition of Systems Engineering in connection with Project Management]{Decomposition of Systems Engineering in connection with Project Management \autocite{sheaNASASystemsEngineering2020}}
	\label{fig:i_nasa_sye_decomposition}
\end{figure}

The space engineering community has used several prominent frameworks for the realisation of complex projects, which may cover, for example, \emph{needs definitions}, \emph{lifecycle approaches}, \emph{engineering/data management} or \emph{system architecture definition} \autocite{sebok}. Some of the most popular examples are:
\begin{itemize}
	\item 
 Separation of a project into separate stages, from conception to end-of-life, following a ``waterfall'' model
 \autocite{ECSS-M-ST-10C,sheaNASASystemsEngineering2020}
 \item 
Requirements Engineering, where specific technical specifications are set for the systems and its components \autocite{ECSS-E-ST-10-06C} 
\item The V-model, which defines a sequential, stage-gated process to implement and verify a system with its components
\autocite{bundesrepublikdeutschlandVModellXT2006,sebok}
\item Iterative lifecycle models, where iteration or evolution cycles are added to allow easy implementation of lessons learned
\autocite{larmanIterativeIncrementalDevelopment2003,sebok_incremental}
\item Concurrent Engineering, where a design is progressed synchronously by multiple engineers \autocite{bandecchiConcurrentEngineeringApplied1999}
\item Model-based Systems Engineering, where formalised models are used to support Systems Engineering tasks \autocite{sebok,hendersonValueBenefitsModelbased2021}
\end{itemize}

\begin{marginfigure}
	\includegraphics{media/drafts/nasa_sye_benefit.png}
	\caption[It is harder to reverse design decisions, the more effort has been dedicated to a project]{It is harder to reverse design decisions, the more effort has been dedicated to a project. Taken from \cite{sheaNASASystemsEngineering2020}}
\end{marginfigure}
The value and benefits of Systems Engineering are asserted by the ``\acs{SYE} hypothesis'': that additional effort spent in Systems Engineering, especially early in the project, will increase the overall \emph{value} of it, especially in terms of cost, schedule and objectives \autocite{honourUnderstandingValueSystems2004}. A 1992 survey of 32 \acs{NASA} programs, for example, shows a correlation between time spent in the design phases of a project, and reduced final cost overruns \cite{honourUnderstandingValueSystems2004,stutzkeEstimatingSoftwareintensiveSystems2005}. 
Various other surveys have shown similar correlations, demonstrating the value of Systems Engineering 
\autocite{sheaNASASystemsEngineering2020,francoisSystemicsCyberneticsHistorical1999}.

The specific constraints of a project which measure its success and resource utilisation are elaborated by \citeauthor{akbarStatisticalAnalysisEffects2018} \autocite{akbarStatisticalAnalysisEffects2018}, who follow the \emph{six-pointed star model}\footnote{Originally inspired by the cost-time-scope project management triangle} enumerating the following constraints that need to be balanced in a project: \textbf{schedule, scope, budget, risk, resources and quality}. An elaboration of these factors is shown in \Cref{tab:i_success_factors}.

Specifically for university spacecraft, \citeauthor{swartwoutUniversityClassSpacecraft2023202023} notes that projects with fixed delivery times often fail to plan and prepare for the design and complexity of their systems and to devote enough resources to systems-level testing, and cites this as a main reason for a high mission failure rate \autocite{swartwoutUniversityClassSpacecraft20232023}. Interviews conducted by \citeauthor{campoModelbasedSystemsEngineering2023,honore-livermoreAcademicsPerceptionSystems2022} also note the benefits of \acs{SYE} and \acs{SYE} approaches in improving communication, project understanding and verification capabilities \autocite{campoModelbasedSystemsEngineering2023,honore-livermoreDigitalEngineeringDevelopment2022}.

%\citeauthor{stutzkeEstimatingSoftwareintensiveSystems2005} found a correlation between more time spent on Systems Engineering for \acs{NASA} projects before 1995 and reduced cost overruns \autocite{stutzkeEstimatingSoftwareintensiveSystems2005}.

However, a considerable overhead and a number of inefficiencies are added when following highly detailed or bureaucratic processes. \citeauthor{royceManagingDevelopmentLarge1970} \autocite{royceManagingDevelopmentLarge1970} famously criticised the ``waterfall'' model mainly because of its inflexibility towards design and requirements changes, and incorrect implementations of the lifecycle model. During the 1990s, these so-called ``heavyweight'' methods started receiving more criticism, and being seen as an impediment for development of lower-scale products \autocite{fowlerNewMethodology2005}. 
%\citeauthor{whitneyWhyMechanicalDesign1996} \autocite{whitneyWhyMechanicalDesign1996} draws parallels between the design of a complex mechanical system, and very efficient and modular VLSI design process.

\citeauthor{boehmROISystemsEngineering2008} \autocite{boehmROISystemsEngineering2008}
analysed the 161 software projects from the \acs{COCOMO} II database to identify the ideal \textbf{trade-off between time spent architecting and total time spent} for a project.
They identified a ``\textbf{sweet spot}'' between too little and too much time spent in software architecting and risk management\footnote{Which, for the purposes of this work, loosely corresponds to effort spent for Systems Engineering}.
For example, on a \SI{10}{\kilo\acs{SLOC}} project, any additional time spent for Systems Engineering above 5\% of the total project time leads to an increase to overall project duration.
This is in contrast to a larger \SI{1000}{\kilo\acs{SLOC}} project, where this sweet spot is found at 33\%, implying that larger projects need disproportionately higher effort for Systems Engineering (\Cref{fig:i_software_roi}).
Similarly, \autocite{honourTechnicalReportValue2004} concluded, after a survey of 44 projects, that an optimum level of \acs{SYE} effort is between \qtyrange{15}{20}{\percent}, above which there seem to be no \acs{SYE}-related cost/schedule improvements.

	\label{fig:i_software_roi}
\end{marginfigure}

The above results show that an adequate balance needs to be found when adopting a \acl{SYE} methodology for a \draft{project}. \citeauthor{akbarStatisticalAnalysisEffects2018} classified \acs{SYE} methodologies into \emph{heavy-weight} ones, which are typically process and tool-oriented, comprehensive and predictive, and \emph{light-weight} ones, which are more empirical, collaborative, and decentralised \autocite{akbarStatisticalAnalysisEffects2018}. Their survey of 20 \draft{software} organisations matches the expectation that the ``scale'' of a methodology should be adapted to the size of a project to reach the best possible benefits.

\draft{In order to identify the research gaps in the understanding of \acl{SYE} as applied to \acf{SCS}, it is first important to investigate the state-of-the-art for \acs{SCS} design.}

The impact and benefit of Systems Engineering is widely understood in the fields of space systems, and more specifically, \textbf{CubeSat engineering}. Apart from individual missions that explore tailor-made \acl{SYE} approaches \autocite{cappaertBuildingDeployingOperating2018,chaiebOpenOrbiterCubeSatSystemofsystems2015,dallmannAgileSpaceParadigm2015,lillAgileMissionOperations2018,lubian-arenillasNanosatelliteDevelopmentMethodology2019,segretPavingStonesInitial2014}, research has been made on more generic space methodologies that can be universally applied to nano-satellite projects \autocite{alanaziEngineeringMethodologyStudentDriven2019,andersonEnterpriseModelingCubeSats2014,deckerSystemsengineeringAssessmentMultiple2016}. These methodologies generally use a foundation based on traditional phase-based lifecycle processes \cite{sheaNASASystemsEngineering2020}, augmented with iterative or incremental elements. Many CubeSat methodologies also implement Agile practices, which represent a philosophy focused on emergent and interpersonal methods \autocite{beckAgileManifesto2001}. Conversely, another set of CubeSat projects does not use a well-defined methodology, instead implementing original ad-hoc approaches applicable to specific missions \todo{[cite]}.

The development approaches for current \acf{SCS}, mainly PocketQubes and ChipSats, largely follow the aforementioned approaches for CubeSats, with examples following hybrid \todo{[cite]}, Agile-based \autocite{sperettaCubeSatsPocketQubesOpportunities2016} and ad-hoc \autocite{manchesterCentimeterScaleSpacecraftDesign2015,daradimosQUBIK1pPocketQube2024} methodologies.

Indeed, both nano-satellites and smaller classes can be classified as \emph{(very) small spacecraft}, and usually have similar high-level requirements. These requirements revolve both around the function of the devices (to reliably and autonomously operate one or \draft{a few} payloads in \acl{LEO}) and the programmatic constraints (such launch deadline, limited budget, small team size)\footnote{Non-examples that may not fit some of these criteria include: human spaceflight or massive constellations.}. These similarities can also extend over non-spacecraft projects, such as hosted payloads or scientific balloon/sounding rocket missions\footnote{An example of a sub-orbital initiatiative is \acs{ESA}'s REXUS/BEXUS programme, where teams are given 8--16 months to develop a scientific payload \cite{REXUSBEXUSSchedule}.}.

Based on these similarities and the number of successes of launched \acs{SCS} missions so far, one can claim that these methodologies maintain their validity in terms of project quality. Do they, however, maintain their validity in terms of the rest of \Cref{tab:i_success_factors}'s factors, such as schedule and budget? Or, \draft{worded differently}, are these methodologies still \emph{effective} and \emph{optimal} for \acs{SCS} projects?

\citeauthor{heinAttoSatsChipSatsOther2019} posited, for example, that atto-satellites offer characteristics that make them possible to develop (from conceptualisation to flight hardware) within 6 months, enabling mass accessibility \todo{and more good stuff} \autocite{heinAttoSatsChipSatsOther2019}. \todo{Blablabla}

What are the specific characteristics of \acs{SCS} that differentiate them from CubeSats and justify the usage of a different methodology?
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Size and mass}: The mass of a single pocket-sized spacecraft can be \( 1 /2 \)\textsuperscript{nd} to \( 1/200 \)\textsuperscript{th} of a CubeSat, with the volume following a similar trend. The first implication of this constraint is a significantly reduced launch cost, which can affect \begin{enumerate*}
		\item whether an organisation can launch any spacecraft at all;
		\item how often an organisation can launch spacecraft;
		\item how many spacecraft can be launched in a single time period.
	\end{enumerate*}

	Another implication of a smaller footprint is less available real-estate for subsystems and components. In turn, this means that \begin{enumerate*}
		\item some components have to be further miniaturised, increasing difficulty or complexity;
		\item space for fewer components and therefore reduced functionality compared to larger systems;
		\item more difficulty applying concepts that require space, such as modularity or redundancy.
	\end{enumerate*}

	Finally, the physical constraints related to reduced space can limit the performance of the system and its subsystems. Most notably, a system's energy generation, energy storage and communication capabilities are directly tied to the solar panel area, battery volume and effective antenna areas respectively. Other effects can \draft{include} thermal management, attitude control or propulsion.

	While the above are the direct implications of size and mass, as the main differentiating factors, they also indirectly affect other aspects of a project, which are mentioned \draft{below}.
	\item \textbf{Facility requirements}: Some of the most expensive, hard-to-maintain and critical equipment for integration and verification of CubeSat projects needs to support the sizes of 6U or larger spacecraft. This can include clean rooms, \ac{TVAC} chambers, or \draft{vibration shakers}. A smaller physical size of a device under manufacturing or test corresponds to reduced requirements for this equipment as well.
	\item \textbf{Low mass / area ratio}: Specifically for flat spacecraft, such as ChipSats, one dimension can be much smaller than the other two. This more assymetric shape increases the average drag coefficient of the system, as well as the \draft{thermally radiative dissipation area}.
	
	The latter characteristic hastens thermal equilibrium in the hot and cold cases. Combined with the potentially increased difficulty of thermal control due to size, this characteristic discourages the use of thermally-sensitive payloads. From a \acl{SYE} point of view, it makes the use of conventional batteries difficult, as they cannot be charged or discharged below a specific temperature. \draft{Manchester} \todo{[cite]} performed a thermal analysis of of the Sprite ChipSats, \draft{finding that they can reach temperatures down to \qty{-100}{\degreeCelsius} in eclipse.} These low numbers also prevent the operation of most commercial electronics.

	A higher drag coefficient will also lead to a faster decay of the spacecraft, reducing its mission life. \draft{Manchester} \todo{[cite]} calculated that a \draft{XXX km} Sprite launch has an expected lifetime of \draft{XX days}, significantly shorter than the few years calculated for CubeSats\footnote{Even though the re-entry time is also a factor of launch altitude, this parameter cannot be usually controlled by customers, and lies in the range between \qtyrange{500}{550}{\kilo\meter} for \acsp{SSO}.}.
	\item \textbf{Low technical size}: Intuitively, a smaller number of components and subsystems should lead to a smaller number of functions that can be performed by the system, as well as fewer interfaces that need to be managed\footnote{See \Cref{sec:INTRODUCTION_metrics} for the definition the technical size and complexity metrics.}. In turn, the reduced number of physical components, functions and interfaces reduces the amount of effort required in design, development and integration. This renders \acs{SCS} more ``light-weight'' spacecraft compared to their larger counterparts, and also reduces the amount of resources required for these projects, making them potentially ``small-scale''.
	
	At the same time, however, the required miniaturisation of components can increase complexity for specific applications, such as deployment mechanisms and thrusters, because of technical manufacturing and fabrication challenges related to the need for increased precision \todo{[cite?]}. \draft{The construction of miniaturised hardware can prevent the use of a manual approach, requiring special machinery, and components that are not available commercially.}
	\item \textbf{\acs{COTS} availability}: Due to both the small size and the lower popularity of \acs{SCS} compared to CubeSat and larger spacecraft, the number of suitable \acf{COTS} components tailored for use in space is limited, especially when considering high-\acs{TRL} \draft{components}. Teams will therefore be more likely to design or develop subsystems and parts in-house. This characteristic can also extend to support equipment, such as FlatSat supports, test jigs, or analysis software.
	
	Similarly, the novelty of PocketQubes, and more-so smaller femtosatellites and attosatellites, also \draft{limits} the amount of standardisation and established conventions in this field. In practical terms, fewer deployer and interface designs may be available as specifications or physical implementations. Especially for ChipSat-scale spacecraft, the different proposed applications \draft{require diversity in form factors, making future standardisation potentially harder than on their larger counterparts.} However, the overall physical structure of many \acs{SCS} projects can be described in simple terms: either as a stack of \acsp{PCB} supported by a small frame (PocketQubes), or as one or two flat \acsp{PCB} with minimal support (ChipSats).
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure*}
	\includegraphics{graphics/Methodoconcept.pdf}
	\caption{Mapping between novel \acs{SCS} characteristics (compared to CubeSats) and \draft{opportunities} for a \acs{SYE} methodology}
	\label{fig:i_important_mapping}
\end{figure*}
The above characteristics explain differences between CubeSats and \acs{SCS} which inform some of the key decisions taken in the \acl{SYE} \draft{process}. Taking this argument one step further, they can directly motivate different shifts in established CubeSat development methodologies and approaches as follows:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Once a design has been elaborated, the development, manufacturing, assembly and integration of either a subsystem, or the complete spacecraft, can be performed in a much shorter time. In the extreme case of ChipSats, this production chain involves the procurement of a \acs{PCB} and component assembly, which can last 2 weeks or less. Likewise, the material cost for an entire satellite model can drop down to a few hundred euros \todo{[cite]}. This observation encourages mass iteration cycles on a subsystem or even system level.
	
	\item A very constrainted architecture might prevent the implementation of concepts and design approaches that would further constrain it, such as Separation of Concerns, modularity, or reusability.

	\item There is a natural tendency to shift both manufacturing and verification in-house, due to the reduction in the requirements for expertise and tooling. \draft{Instead of a} cleanroom requirement, teams can comfortably work on a flow bench. If thermal chambers, \draft{electrodynamic shakers}, Helmholtz cages or other testing equipment is needed, its scale can be reduced. This makes it possible for developers to own and operate manufacturing and testing equipment very close to the \draft{daily working location}, limiting costs, time spent, adminsitrative burden, and making \draft{very short manufacturing runs and test campaigns} possible.
	
	\item While \acs{SCS} projects still retain mission-criticality, since irreperable failures can propagate and lead to a loss of mission, the consequences of failure are usually less severe. \todo{in terms of what?} Additionally, shorter mission durations \draft{mean} that  that accumulating effects (such as those related to radiation or aging) are less \draft{severe}\footnote{Note, however, that most CubeSat mission failures happen at the beginning of a mission, as a result of ``infant mortality'' \todo{[cite swartwout]}}. As a result, developers need to take fewer derating and techincal risk mitigration measures, such as the use of radiation-tolerant components or redundancy. Administrative measures for technical risk reduction (such as \acs{FMEA} \todo{or fuzz testing}) can also be \draft{potentially reduced in fidelity}.
	
	\item While CubeSat projects have a fixed set of launch requirements, originating from the launcher, the deployer and the CubeSat specification, non-standardised projects may require the implementation of proprietary interfaces, potentially specific to each company that can offer a flight slot for \acs{SCS} spacecraft. In extreme cases, these interface specifications may not have been devised at all. Then, \acs{SCS} developers will have to collaborate with their provider to agree on shared interfaces, or design their own\footnote{This was, for example, the approach taken by Manchester, who designed a 3U CubeSat capable of hosting and deploying multiple ChipSats \cite{manchesterCentimeterScaleSpacecraftDesign2015}.}. These uncertainties increase the possibility of external requirements changing during a mission.
	
	\item The reduced launch costs, sizes, and number of components, combined with the \draft{popularity} of swarm-based or distributed \acs{SCS} concepts, greatly favour the development and launch of multiple similar spacecraft together, sometimes in the form of constellations. This approach adds a form of ``operational reliability'', since a launch of multiple spacecraft can be considered as a \draft{redundancy strategy}.
	
	\item The constrained power generation and storage capabilities of \acs{SCS}, combined with more limiting earth communication capabilities\footnote{Influenced not only by the available energy, but also by antenna size}, enforce different approaches for \acf{CONOPS}. While data transmission is expensive, Moore's law has successfully predicted significant processing capabilities with reduced power consumption and real estate \todo{[cite]}, encouraging the processing of data ``on-the-edge''\footnote{that is, on the spacecraft instead of the ground station} \autocite{moreiraEdgeComputingSpace2025}. 
\end{itemize}

The above opportunities and possible decisions demostrate that a distinct, tailored methodology for \acs{SCS} can be followed. What would be, however, the potential effects of such a methodology? In \Cref{tab:i_success_factors} I listed the ``success factors'' of Systems Engineering for a project, which include schedule, cost, risk, scope, resources and quality. For a proposed methodology to be considered useful, it must therefore:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=-1ex]
	\item Demonstrate \textbf{internal and external validity}, showing that it can indeed be used to bring an \acs{SCS} project from conceptualisation to qualification/launch.
	\item Demonstrate that its effects on one or more of the aforementioned success criteria are \textbf{significant} compared to other environmental, programmatic or technical factors.
	\item Demonstrate that it has \textbf{improved performance}\todo{?} compared to current State-of-the-Art approaches for pocket-sized spacecraft, in terms of one or more of these factors.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{enumerate}

	\item Identify quantifiable parameters that can describe shortcomings in current \ac{SYE} approaches
	\item Synthesise a set of methods for technical management and system realisation that describe a complete \ac{SYE} methodology for \ac{SCS}
	\item Validate the new methodology on at least one space mission that will succeed in environmental qualification
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Spacecraft Classes}
\label{sec:LITERATURE_classes}

%The idea of a \textbf{Sub-CubeSat Spacecraft} (\acs{SCS}) depends on the definition of a CubeSat

The CubeSat Design Specification \autocite{CDS14} defines a CubeSat as a class of satellite composed of 1 to 12 cube units with a size of \SI{10}{\centi\meter}  (``U''). As such, I define \textbf{\acf{SCS}} to include all spacecraft classes smaller than \SI{10}{\centi\meter} on all dimensions. Depending on launch requirements, this definition will usually restrict an \acs{SCS} mass to less than \SI{2}{\kg}, the maximum mass for a 1U CubeSat \cite{CDS14}.

Various \acl{SCS} classes have been defined in the literature. Most prominently, \textbf{PocketQubes} are standardised in the PocketQube Standard \autocite{raduDelfiPQFirstPocketqube2018} as combinations of \SI{5}{\centi\meter} cube units (``p''). Smaller sizes are represented by classes such as \textbf{\acs{PCB}sats} \autocite{barnhartLowcostFemtosatelliteEnable2009}, which are typically flat in one direction, and \textbf{SpaceChips} or satellites-on-a-chip \autocite{barnhartSatelliteonaChipFeasibilityStudy2006}, which would be complete spacecraft implemented on an \acf{IC} smaller than \qtyproduct{2 x 2}{\centi\meter}. The notion of a \textbf{ChipSat} can define a flat-sized spacecraft with an envelope smaller than \qtyproduct{5 x 5}{\centi\meter} \autocite{manchesterCentimeterScaleSpacecraftDesign2015}.

``Fractional-unit CubeSats'' have been also developed and launched (e.g.~1/2U, 1/3U, 1/4U)\draft{reference}. While these conform to many standard CubeSat requirements and are designed to be hosted in CubeSat containers, they are not part of the official CubeSat Design Specification \cite{CDS14}, and due to their size, are considered \acl{SCS} and therefore within the scope of this work.

\subsection{Method and Methodology}

As the primary objective of this work is to synthesise a \emph{methodology} for Sub-CubeSat spacecraft, it is important to define the scope of a design \emph{methodology} compared to a \emph{method}, \emph{process} or \emph{approach}.

\citeauthor{gerickeWhatWeNeed2017}'s works \autocite{gerickeWhatWeNeed2017,gerickeElementsDesignMethod2022} investigate the differences between different definitions of the above terms. I adopt the following definitions:

\begin{definition}[Design Method]
	A method is specification of how a specified result is to be achieved. This may include specifications of how information is to be shown, what information is to be used as inputs to the method, what tools are to be used, what actions are to be performed and how, and how the task should be decomposed and how actions should be sequenced. \cite{gerickeWhatWeNeed2017}
\end{definition}

A complete description of a design method must include its core idea, its representation of the information the method produces, a procedure (set of actions or activities to be followed), and its intended use \autocite{gerickeElementsDesignMethod2022}. As tools are given specific attention in my research, I further reproduce their definition here:
\begin{definition}[Tool]
	A tool is an object or artefact that is used to perform some action [...]. For design methods, documents or software that direct and structure designers' thoughts and actions, or facilitate constructing representations, or perform computations. \cite{gerickeElementsDesignMethod2022} \todo{something is wrong here}
\end{definition}

The term \emph{methodology} itself lacks a universally agreed-upon definition, often being used interchangeably with ``method''. For this work, I will adopt part of the definition provided by \autocite{pahlIntroduction2007}:

\begin{definition}[Design Methodology]
	A design methodology is a concrete course of action for the design of technical systems that derives its knowledge from design science and cognitive psychology, and from practical experience in different domains. It includes plans of action that link working steps and design phases according to content and organisation. These plans must be adapted in a flexible manner to the specific task at hand. It also includes strategies, rules and principles to achieve general and specific goals as well as methods to solve individual design problems or partial tasks.
\end{definition}

In the general sense, a methodology can be then seen as a collection of methods used for the design and development of a system.

\subsection{Femto-satellites and atto-satellites}

The earliest ideas about spacecraft miniaturisation to a mass below \SI{100}{\gram} started from attempts to miniaturise satellite avionics, as shown by \citeauthor{alkalaiIntegratedMicrospacecraftAvionics1996}'s \autocite{alkalaiIntegratedMicrospacecraftAvionics1996} 3D \ac{MCM} proposal.

The proposed PalmSat design included solar cells generating \SI{2}{\watt} of power, a PIC microcontroller, a magnetorquer and reaction-wheel-based \acs{ADCS}, and space for a camera-based remote-sensing payload.

\citeauthor{barnhartSatelliteonaChipFeasibilityStudy2006} \autocite{barnhartSatelliteonaChipFeasibilityStudy2006} proposed another conceptual design for a ``satellite-on-a-chip''. Perhaps the first launch and operation of centimetre-scale spacecraft was achieved by Zac Manchester, as a part of his work in Cornell University.
Manchester designed ``KickSat'', a crowd-funded mission to design and launch multiple ChipSat-class spacecraft \autocite{manchesterKickSatCrowdFundedMission2013,manchesterCentimeterScaleSpacecraftDesign2015}. ``KickSat'' was also the name given to the 3U CubeSat that would launch the ChipSats, which themselves retained the ``Sprite'' name.

The number of Sprites that could fit into a single CubeSat meant brought the effective Sprite cost close to \SI{300}{USD}\footnote{Although the crowdfunding campaign expected a free launch}, allowing supporters to purchase ``their own Sprite in space with [their] initials in its radio transmissions'' \draft{cite kickstarter}.

The actual launch of the KickSat-1 spacecraft took place in April 2014, however, due to anomalies in the CubeSat, the Sprite deployment failed.
The Sprite design was tested once again in space, as a secondary payload of the Venta-1 mission in 2017, with a successful demonstration \autocite{adamsTheoryApplicationsGramScale2020}.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:LITERATURE_metrics}

\citeauthor{honourUnderstandingValueSystems2004} \autocite{honourUnderstandingValueSystems2004} provides a list of quantifiable parameters that can gauge the effect of Systems Engineering:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Project Duration}: The overall time between the initial conceptualisation until the validation of a product. In aerospace, ``validation'' can be taken to mean environmental testing and qualification, or an actual flight of a project.
	\item \textbf{Project Cost}: In the context of aerospace systems, this can include launch costs, personnel costs, material costs, and further development costs. We should note that academic projects often include volunteer engineers, resulting in lower or zero personnel costs.
	\item \textbf{Risk}: Risk can be understood in a technical (e.g.~a hazard or probability of system failure) or programmatic (e.g.~risk of cost/schedule overruns) sense.
	\acs{ECSS} defines risk as an ``undesirable situation or circumstance that has both a likelihood of occurring and a potential negative consequence on a project'' \autocite{ECSS-S-ST-00-01C}.
	\citeauthor{honourUnderstandingValueSystems2004} \cite{honourUnderstandingValueSystems2004} provides a mathematical definition for risk \(r\): \[
	r = c_{90\%} - \bar{c}
	\]
	where \( \bar{c} \) is the average expected cost of the project, and \( c_{90\%} \) the maximum cost that 90\% of identical projects would experience.
	\item \textbf{Technical Quality}: A comparison between the actual resulting system and the intended objective.
	A simple mathematical definition of quality can be constructed as: \[
		q = \frac{\text{\# Project objectives met by actual system}}{\text{\# Original project objectives}}
	\]
\end{itemize}

It is also important to define the \textbf{attributes} of a project, which can determine what kind of \acs{SYE} frameworks, approaches and decisions can be followed.

\citeauthor{honourUnderstandingValueSystems2004} \autocite{honourUnderstandingValueSystems2004} defines two main quantifiable attributes:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Technical Size}, representing the overall size of the development effort.
	Some measures can be the number of requirements, number of components, or number of functions.
	\item \textbf{Technical Complexity}, representing the required degree of interaction between components, independent from size.
\end{itemize}

On the other hand, \citeauthor{kalusCriteriaSoftwareProcess2013} \autocite{kalusCriteriaSoftwareProcess2013} have identified 127 different criteria for choosing a software process, which can be extrapolated for use in aerospace \acs{SYE}. These criteria are classified into the following categories:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Team}: Personal and collective characteristics of the people involved in a project, including experience, size and distribution;
	\item \textbf{Internal Environment}: The available tools, decisions, and current situation of a project, including budget, duration, and management philosophy;
	\item \textbf{External Environment}: The project effects of external factors, such as stakeholders, clients and users;
	\item \textbf{Objectives}: System design imperatives and decisions that affect the project, including the domain, complexity and required innovations.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Life Cycle Models}
The selection of a life cycle model for the development phases is a key decision in systems engineering.
The \ac{SEBOK} wiki defines a life cycle as ``a series of stages [\textellipsis] which confirm that the system is mature enough to leave one stage and enter another'' \autocite{sebok}.
\marginnote{For this work, when using the term ``life cycle'', I refer to the life cycle of the \emph{project}, and not the \emph{system} or an instance of the system. The former includes all project stages, from initial ideation to operations, while the latter refers to the stages in the life of a physical system, such as commissioning, \draft{main mission operations}, and disposal.} %This is also where terms like "infant mortality" come into place, see Bathtub curve.
A life cycle model is individual and specific to each project, but usually follows a pre-specified life cycle \emph{process model}. 
\acs{SEBOK} categories these models into three categories:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Pre-specified and sequential processes (such as the waterfall model)
	\item Evolutionary and concurrent processes (such as the V-model and spiral model)
	\item Interpersonal and emergent processes (such as agile development and scrum)
\end{enumerate}
\acs{SEBOK} also provides a ``generic life cycle model'', which serves as a starting point for lifecycle processes, and includes the main stages of
system definition, conceptualisation, realisation, production and utilisation. 

\begin{figure}[h]
	\includegraphics{graphics/ProcessModelExamples.pdf}
	\caption[Typical lifecycle process models]{Typical lifecycle process models \autocite{sebok_drivers}}
	\label{fig:l_sebok_models}
\end{figure}

\acs{SEBOK} defines an additional partition within into \emph{Single-Step} and \emph{Multi-Step} processes.
Single-Step models expect no development iterations, while Multi-Step models split the development, usually by repeating some stages of the process in an iterative or incremental fashion.
\citeauthor{HEEAGER201822} \autocite{HEEAGER201822} explains the differences between ``incremental'' and ``iterative'' lifecycles:
Incremental development defines an approach where complete versions (\emph{increments}) of a system are produced as part of repeated lifecycle stages,
while iterative development refers to an approach where different \emph{pieces} of the system are reworked one at a time.
Hybrid approaches that combine incremental and iterative development are also observed \autocite{garzanitiHybridAgileProduct2020,carpenterAgileTooFragile2014}.

Traditional space systems engineering has traditionally followed a V or waterfall-type process \autocite{ECSS-M-ST-10C,sheaNASASystemsEngineering2020}, with well-defined stage-gates between different project stages.
Project phases are typically separated into Phase 0 (concept studies/mission analysis/needs identification), Phase A (feasibility/concept development/technology development), Phase B (preliminary definition), Phase C (detailed definition/fabrication), Phase D (assembly/qualification), Phase E (operations) and Phase F (close-out/disposal) \cite{ECSS-M-ST-10C,sheaNASASystemsEngineering2020}.
This approach arises from the high costs associated with early space systems engineering, and the risk-averse attitude of developers, especially in the case of human spaceflight.
Indeed, \draft{a significant time spent during the early definition phases combined with a rigorous review process has been shown to reduce later mission cost, risk, and delays} \todo{citation please - check sebok\_value}.
However, this paradigm has often come under challenge, with different missions taking incremental or iterative approaches to system design \todo{citation please/carpenterAgileTooFragile2014}, the most notable example being SpaceX's ``move fast and break things'' approach \todo{CITATION!}
\acs{SEBOK} claims that the integrated and interactive nature of modern hardware-software systems has rendered pre-specified processes obsolete, as system interfaces and behaviours can emerge better with their use, rather than extensive and bureaucratic processes.


On the other hand, it is possible for engineering projects to be associated with no predetermined lifecycle process.
These projects can be said to follow a ``by-default'' or ``ad-hoc'' model \autocite{cowper941SystemsEngineering2004}.
This is often the case for small, volunteer-based or ``\ac{DIY}'' projects \autocite{bohmerOpenInnovationEcosystem}. However, even if no formal description is provided for the lifecycle used, the above models can often describe the actual work process followed. Simple projects follow a single-step process, which can evolve into a multi-step process with an undetermined number of steps \todo{citation needed}, with the benefits of systems engineering and a predetermined methodology becoming clear as the project scale grows (\Cref{sec:defining_systems_engineering}).

\paragraph{Agile Frameworks}
\label{par:LITERATURE_agile_frameworks}

While a number of different Agile frameworks have been proposed, only a small number of them are in effective use in the industry as of 2025.
Scrum and \acf{SAFE} are the two most popular formal frameworks in use, with developers also creating ad-hoc methods based on agile principles \autocite{digital.aiStateAgileReport2025}.
A \citeyear{2020} survey by \citeauthor{atzbergerAgileDevelopmentPhysical2020} \autocite{atzbergerAgileDevelopmentPhysical2020} of hardware developers found that \qty{90}{\percent} of the respondents used a form of Scrum as a lifecycle process model, while other methods, such as \emph{Kanban} and \emph{Design Thinking}, also enjoy some popularity.

In this section, I will analyse the most popular Agile frameworks and evaluate their applicability to hardware applications, and more specifically the \ac{SCS} methodology.

\subparagraph{Scrum}
\label{sec:LITERATURE_scrum}
\marginnote{Scrum's authors claim it was derived from complex systems theory, and influenced by best practices and research results from Japanese and USA-based companies in the end of the 20\textsuperscript{th} century. They describe it as a ``pattern language for hyperproductive software development'' \autocite{sutherlandNUTBOLTSORIGINS}. The name ``Scrum'' originated from rugby football terminology, where the term signifies players packing closely together to gain control of the ball \autocite{sutherlandNUTBOLTSORIGINS}.}
Scrum, one of the most popular agile frameworks in use, was developed by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland and covers management practices during the entire life cycle of a software project \autocite{boehmBalancingAgilityDiscipline2004}. Scrum is primarily defined by ``The Definitive Guide to Scrum'' by \citeauthor{schwaverDefinitiveGuideScrum2020} \autocite{schwaverDefinitiveGuideScrum2020}, which defines the theory and processes behind the framework.

The framework is built around fixed-time iterations called \emph{Sprints}, which follow a repeated pattern of \emph{planning-standup-review-retrospective} with the aim of presenting a complete product at the end of each iteration. It also defines the different terminology and roles within small development team.

With its main description being no more than 14 pages long, Scrum is considered a light-weight framework, and can therefore be easily applied or tailored for different organisations. Popular generic tailorings include \acf{LESS} and \emph{Scrum@Scale}, variants designed for organisations with multiple Scrum-based teams that may be building one or more products \autocite{larmanLargescaleScrumMore2017}.

\subparagraph{Extreme Programming (XP)}
Extreme Programming is a software development methodology, similar to Scrum, which combines different methods and guidelines that particularly apply to software development.
Typical aspects of XP include having a customer representative continuously present on-site, iterations lasting three weeks or less, pair programming, code reviews, frequent refactoring, test-driven development, and continuous integration. Despite its initial popularity, it received criticism due to its environment-specific assumption, and difficulties in scaling \autocite{boehmBalancingAgilityDiscipline2004}. \citeauthor{atzbergerAgileDevelopmentPhysical2020}'s survey shows that it was used by \qty{7}{\percent} of industrial developers \autocite{atzbergerAgileDevelopmentPhysical2020}.

\subparagraph[SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework)]{\acf{SAFE}}

The \acl{SAFE} is another popular set of organisational practices, designed to scale Agile across organisations consisting of multiple teams. It includes concepts such as \emph{Lean Portfolio Management} to guide an organisation's strategy, it guides the product integration and delivery flow based on Scrum-like iterations, and it provides guidance for leadership and organisational structure. Its primary concept is the \emph{``Release Train''}, a team-of-teams with more than 50 individuals that share a common goal. As a scaled framework, it mainly targets organisations with more than 250--1000 developers \autocite{heusserIntroducingScaledAgile2015}. Common criticisms against \acl{SAFE} address its rigidity and strict hierarchical structure, as well as its dependence on predictability, which are said to only create an illusion of implementing Agile \autocite{schwaberUnsafeAnySpeed2013}.

\subparagraph{Miscellaneous Frameworks}
Most other documented Agile frameworks are not reported to be as popular, but can still provide an interesting source of \draft{inputs}.

DAD (Disciplined Agile Delivery), introduced in 2013, is a set of guidelines for Agile software organisations that aims to encapsulate other, more fundamental Agile frameworks \autocite{ChooseYourWoW}. \subparagraph{Miscellaneous Methods}
Apart from frameworks that cover the entire structure of a team, project, or organisation, there also exist specific methods and tools which can be categorised as Agile but cover only specific parts of project management or software development.

\paragraph{Hardware-specific Frameworks}
Because of the introduction of Agile methods int hardware development, a number of hardware-specific recommendations or frameworks have also been proposed for different scales of physical systems. However, there is no single hardware-specific framework that enjoys considerably widespread adoption \autocite{atzbergerAgileDevelopmentPhysical2020}, with the field being supported instead by various proprietary approaches. \draft{Nevertheless}, most developers tailor software-based Agile ideas for their use \autocite{cooperAgileDevelopmentManufacturing2023}.

\subparagraph{Agile-Stage-Gate}
\citeauthor{cooperAgileDevelopmentManufacturing2023} \autocite{cooperAgileDevelopmentManufacturing2023} observed an emergent hybrid model that is used by physical product developers, which replaces traditional project management methods with Scrum, but maintains traditional stages and gates \emph{``for strategic and directional reasons''}. In contrast to Scrum, iterations can last between two weeks and three months, stand up meetings are only held two--three times per week, and each iteration has the goal of presenting a ``demo'' to management or customer.

\subparagraph{Slice-Based Integration}
\citeauthor{radekaWhenAgileGets2022} \autocite{radekaWhenAgileGets2022,iberleAgileGetsPhysical2022} also propose a Scrum-based framework, based on the concept of a \textbf{slice}, which is an independently verifiable feature or set of features of a product\footnote{Also see \Cref{def:slice}.}. Then, instead of working on the entire product, teams have a clear set of functionality that needs to be achieved at the end of every timebox. This purportedly helps coordinating hardware and software development. Likewise for verification, different test sets are defined and distributed across iterations.

\chapter{Research Approach}
\label{chap:RESEARCH_APPROACH}

Research Design calls for a selection between a qualitative and quantitative approach \autocite{creswellResearchDesignQualitative2023}. A common issue in engineering research is the difficulty of applying and investigating a study's results in the real world, preventing the generation of statistical data based on experimentation. This lack of numeric data calls for an application of a mixed or qualitative research approach.

Qualitative research typically follows approaches such as narrative, phenomenological, grounded theory, action or case-study research \autocite{creswellQualitativeInquiryResearch2025}. 
For this work, we will follow
the DRM Design Research Methodology \autocite{blessingDRMDesignResearch2009} by \citeauthor{blessingDRMDesignResearch2009}.

In engineering research, DRM is a widely adopted research approach that proposes a plan to create and verify \emph{support}, i.e.~``the possible means, aids and measures that can be used to improve design''. DRM divides the research process into the following four stages:
\begin{enumerate*}
	\item \textbf{Research Clarification (RC)}, where researchers try to find evidence to support their assumptions and research goal, based on a literature review,
	\item \textbf{Descriptive Study I (DS-I)}, where researchers improve their understanding of the research gap and influencing factors through more detailed data gathering,
	\item \textbf{Prescriptive Study (PS)}, where researchers develop and evaluate the new support (such as a methodology, process, method or tool), and
	\item \textbf{Descriptive Study II (DS-II)}, where researchers evaluate the \emph{applicability} and \emph{usefulness} of their new support by further data gathering.
\end{enumerate*}

DRM acknowledges that the comprehensive implementation of all stages can be impractical for a single research project, and proposes different partial plans to cover them. It also allows for parallel execution \emph{iterations} as part of research planning to enable feedback between different stages.

The specific research plan for this work is shown in \draft{Figure Tade} and is elaborated as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Research Clarification (RC)} (Review-based):
	I have performed a literature survey to identify the shortcomings of 
	current Systems Engineering approaches for \ac{SCS}, and an engineering gap that can be resolved through a novel methodology. My survey has focused on available resources (such as time, cost, personnel and technology), goals (such as functionality and reliability), and previous projects.
	
	This is elaborated in \Cref{chap:INTRODUCTION,chap:SURVEY}.
	\item \textbf{Descriptive Study I (DS-I)} (Comprehensive):
	As a first step, I have identified the organisational and technical requirements of typical \acs{SCS} missions, and compared them with the ecosystem of larger space missions. We also analysed the concept of Systems Engineering and identified its components that have both a higher impact on \acs{SCS} mission success, and can be improved through a Systems Engineering methodology. Finally, I conducted informal interviews to validate our findings and the usefulness of our proposed approach.
	This is elaborated in \Cref{chap:SURVEY}.
	
	As a second step, I will participate in the \textbf{design and development of \acs{SCS} space missions} with the objective of \textbf{qualification or flight} as case studies. These space missions will be in the form of payloads, subsystems or satellites that can reach a flight-ready stage. They will be used either as a direct source of information to develop the required support, or as experimental applications of the new proposed methodology, in an iterative fashion.
	
	For this work, four different internal space missions were investigated, as shown in \Cref{sec:ra_casestudies,chap:CASESTUDIES}.
	
	\item
	\textbf{Prescriptive Study (PS)} (Comprehensive):
	Based on the gathered data, I will describe a set of design methods applicable to different components of systems engineering, which combined define a methodology for \acs{SCS}.

	\item

	Validation is usually separated into \emph{internal validity}, which establishes a causal relationship between the premises and results of the proposed support, and \emph{external validity}, which shows whether these results can be generalised \autocite{yinCaseStudyResearch2014}. \todo{Check Yin page 87 for definitions}

\subsection{Requirements: Slices and the MoSCoW method}

The concept of Mission and System Requirements is often the topic of discussion, specifically for CubeSat missions.
In traditional space systems engineering, requirements are often elaborated in significant detail, covering test specifications and guiding all the required features for development. This approach is summarised as one of Akin's laws \autocite{akinAkinLawsSpacecraft}:
\begin{quote}
	Design is based on requirements. There's no justification for designing something one bit "better" than the requirements dictate.
\end{quote}
In these cases, requirements elicitation takes place in the beginning of the project, with little room for changes and modifications at the later phases. \todo{cite} \todo{explain the nature of strictly verifiable and machine-interpretable requirements} \draft{For example, \acs{ECSS} standards forbid the use of words which cannot be determined objectively, such as \emph{minimise/maximise}, \emph{optimise}, ...}

On the other hand, some projects follow less strict approaches, where requirements do not cover the entire system, and are usually elaborated in a more \draft{interpretable} manner.

Especially for \acs{SCS} projects, I have made the following observations:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=-1ex]
	\item \acs{SCS} projects \emph{need} to conform to external requirements to some degree, provided by their host or launch provider.
	\item Due to the lack of standard launch interfaces, specifications and standards, these requirements may change by the relevant stakeholders during the project.
	\item The planned feature-set of a project may change during its course (usually via the removal of functionality) due to incorrect planning, external requirement change or une
	Projects with fixed deadlines will prefer to remove features, instead of adding additional iterations or reworking the product, since the latter may lead to a launch cancellation.
	\item Each project evolves around a central strategic goal that expresses a simple objective (such as \emph{receive at least some data from orbit} or \emph{demonstrate a specific technology}). Additional objectives, when completed, increase the perceived \emph{value} of the project \todo{Ask Alex about the definition of value}.
	\item The definition of \todo{specific} requirements itself as a process can last up to a month, and requires some prior knowledge of the system's principles, feasibility and available technology. In the case of inexperienced teams, external reviews may be necessary.
	\item Well-specified requirements can be the guide for functional and environmental testing campaigns. However, requirements-based testing campaigns cannot offer complete coverage of the executed design, as they do not cover \todo{design-specific} activity flows and edge cases.
\end{itemize}

\draft{I therefore consider it important to define an \acs{SCS}-tailored method for requirements capture} that can still be part of the proposed \emph{project lifecycle approach}, while reducing the \ac{SYE} overhead.

An inspiration from such an approach can come from the \acs{DSDM} Agile framework, which advocates the \textbf{prioritisation} of different project requirements using the \acf{MOSCOW} classification \autocite{agilebusinessconsortiumAgilePMHandbookV22014}, which categorises requirements as follows:
\begin{itemize}
	\item
	\textbf{Must Have} requirements: The Minimum Usable subset of requirements that a project should comply to in order to be legal, safe, compliant with external requests, and functional.
	
	Not complying to these requirements will result in \todo{a cancellation or failure of the project}.
	\item
	\textbf{Should Have} requirements: These represent features or requests that significantly increase project value, but are not project-breaking in case they are not implemented.
	\item \textbf{Could Have} requirements: These represent features that are desirable but less important than \emph{Should Have} ones. They may refer to requests by external entities, features that slightly increase reliability, mission duration or configurability, and generally slightly increase mission value.
	\item \textbf{Won't Have this time} requirements: These requirements represent those that the team has agreed to not implement in a specific iteration. \todo{or version?} This classification usually refers to a reassignment of requirement status. \todo{after a few iterations}
\end{itemize}

While Must Have requirements are a necessity for the flown project, they are not required to demonstrate a complete \emph{functional} model of the product. One can therefore present a further classification of ``must-have'' requirements, as ones that stem from the functionality of the project and have to be present for every \ac{MVP}, and the ones that stem from environmental constraints and need not be implemented for strictly functional models.

In contrast to the traditional approach, \todo{which one?} \acs{MOSCOW} requirements are editable during the development lifecycle of a system. This can take place, for example, during \draft{the sprint planning phase of each iteration}.

\paragraph{Tailoring of The Scrum Process}

\subparagraph{Scrum Team}
Scrum calls for small teams of ``10 or fewer'' people, responsible for all product activities (from conceptualisation to verification) without any additional hierarchies. This matches the typical environment present in \acs{SCS} projects.

While most participants in the team will be developers, responsible for technically advancing each iteration, Scrum specifies the role of a \emph{Product Owner}, responsible for managing and communicating work packages.

The final role defined by the framework is that of the \emph{Scrum Master}, who is responsible for maintaining the Agile processes and coaches the team towards self-management. While it is seen as a critical role by the Scrum guide, it has been by far the most controversial role in \draft{real-world} applications, especially when its responsibilities are not handled correctly, and is usually limited by the team's needs and competencies \autocite{kustersScreamGuide2020,doshiDownfallScrumMaster2025}. As such, I will not include the role of Scrum Master in the tailoring of this methodology -- however, \draft{the roles performed are still necessary}, especially for younger teams that are not experienced in adherence to specific methodologies.

\subparagraph{The Sprint}
Scrum works around the primary concept of a \emph{Sprint}, a  ``fixed event of one month or less'' that has the goal of producing a new iteration of a working product at \draft{its end}. In Scrum, each Sprint starts with a planning phase\footnote{The planning phase aims to answer questions such as why is the Sprint valuable, What can be done during it, and how this work will be done.},  continues with daily time-boxed meetings, and finishes with a review and retrospective\footnote{The difference between \emph{review} and \emph{retrospective} is\draft{...}}

Original Scrum describes a pre-defined sprint duration. However, in hardware-heavy projects, the duration of each iteration is heavily dependent on external and unplannable factors, such as procurement and manufacturing lead times, or component and equipment failures. \citeauthor{douglassAgileSystemsEngineering2015} \autocite{douglassAgileSystemsEngineering2015} advocates varying the iteration length by \( \pm 25\% \) to make sure that a coherent amount of work can be performed. The duration of each Sprint will also be generally larger in hardware-based projects, as it is physically limited by the tasks of procurement and manufacturing, which can take 1 week or more, each. These tasks are also often on the critical path of development, as they prevent the verification and validation of the produced system until they are completed.

This imposition of physical and time-related constraints makes the direct application of Scrum difficult, as it \draft{prevents} the idea of constant work done in parallel. This is identified, for example, in \draft{some paper that I need to find}. However, the context of \acs{SCS} development makes it possible to keep these so-called ``\todo{downtime}'' to a minimum, by proactively reducing external lead time.\marginnote{Reduction of external lead time can be done by:\begin{itemize}
		\item Moving manufacturing in-house, to the degree possible

The selection of a lifecycle process model is one of the most critical decisions that have to be taken early in a project, as it is also the driver for the rest of the management decisions and stakeholder communication.

Gantt charts are a common tool used in project scheduling, showing the durations and dependencies between different parallel activities \autocite{honore-livermoreIntegratingAgileSystems2022}. Gantt charts can be augmented with resource allocation or simplified by showing monthly planning only, as required. \textbf{\ac{EDM}}, \textbf{Technical Data Management} or \textbf{Configuration Management}
is the process of acquiring, accessing, managing and using technical data that describe a system's specifications \autocite{sheaNASASystemsEngineering2020,ECSS-M-ST-40C,ECSS-E-TM-10-25A}. 
The importance of this discipline is shown in the \acs{NASA} Systems Engineering Handbook, explaining that ``[Configuration Management] enables all stakeholders in the technical effort, at any given time in the life of a product, to use identical data for development activities and decision-making'' \cite{sheaNASASystemsEngineering2020}.

In the context of \ac{SCS}, ``Engineering Data'' can specifically mean:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=-2ex]
	\item Requirements specifications and acceptance thresholds
	\item Product trees of the system and subsystem
	\item Interface definitions and specifications between components
	\item System performance parameters (e.g.~mission/phase duration, trajectory)
	\item Subsystem and component physical characteristics (e.g.~mass, dimensions, quantity, location)
	\item Subsystem and component performance characteristics (e.g.~efficiency, power consumption, capacity)
	\item Lists of associated \draft{entities} (e.g.~operational modes, risk matrices, failure modes)
	\item Analysis and test results
	\item Domain-specific models (e.g.~\acs{CAD} models)\footnote{For the purpose of this work, I will not consider domain-specific models, as they are usually managed and accessed through different discipline-specific tools, in contrast to the rest, which can be \draft{essentially represented as plain text}.}
\end{itemize}

It is important to make a distinction between \ac{EDM} and the term ``documentation'', \todo{so let's make it. What we call “Engineering Data” may be combined with project documentation, or be stored in an independent format, in a structured manner or a domain-specific model.}

As Engineering Data is most likely stored and handled in a digital format\footnote{While the use of paper or other physical methods is possible and has been proposed for parts of system design, such as task management \todo{cite kanban/scrum}, I consider here that the typical scope of an \acs{SCS} project includes remote participants and external stakeholders who need access to all engineering data (\Cref{sec:scope}), making their digital representation essentially a requirement.}, \ac{EDM} is essentially a discipline shaped and limited by the available tools.

In general, the success of an \ac{EDM} method does not only depend on the short and long-term correctness of the available data, but should also be focused on minimising the additional \ac{SYE} overhead.
A quantitative model for the evaluation of documentation effectiveness was proposed by \citeauthor{amblerStrategiesAgileSoftware2008} \autocite{amblerStrategiesAgileSoftware2008} as follows:

\]

	\item Produced value is guesstimated by the producers of the artifact.
	\item \(C\) is the percentage of the artifact that is currently “correct”.
	\item \(R\) = The chance that the artifact will be read by the intended audience.
	\item \(U\) = The chance that the document that is actually understood by the intended audience.
	\item \(F\) = The chance that the advice contained in artifact will be followed.

As we lack the statistical data to quantify \ac{EDM} approaches based on this equation, we instead define the following \textbf{criteria for \acl{EDM} processes}:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Correctness}: The System Description should contain factual information at any point in time.
	\item \textbf{Completeness}: The System Description should cover all aspects of the system to the degree of detail required for normal engineering work. This includes information about lower-level components and internal/external interfaces.
	\item \textbf{Efficiency}: The System Description should require a minimal amount of effort and time to produce and maintain.
	\item \textbf{Flexibility}: The System Description should be adaptable to different types of system structures, interfaces, and descriptors \todo{better explanation}
	\item \textbf{Accessibility}: The System Description should be easily readable, and require a minimal amount of time and actions to access the desired specification.
	\item \textbf{Maintainability}: The System Description should provide a way of quickly updating and propagating specification updates, without harming correctness.
\end{enumerate}


Traditionally, the description and documentation of system specifications have taken place via text documents.
The combination of human language with rich text capabilities (e.g.~images, tables, equations, references) makes it possible to represent all \draft{necessary engineering data}. In practical terms, text documents \draft{follow a specific format such as OOXML or MD}. However, the use of prose can quickly become cumbersome as the complexity of a system increases, as it is difficult to represent modular and repeated structures, and the representation of data is not consistent.

		\item \emph{efficiency}, as additions and modifications require tampering with entire documents.
\end{enumerate*}}

In Design Research (\Cref{def:design_research}), a ``design solution'' can be defined as a ``description or set of instructions to make something''\footnote{Also see \Cref{def:design}.} \autocite{loveDefinitionDesign2015}.
From a formal point of view, a Design Space can be defined as the set of all possible design solutions that satisfy a set of requirements; then, the process of Design Space Exploration selects a specific design solution that seems the most ``optimal'' based on project-specific criteria (such as cost, performance, or value). Different methods have been proposed since the mid-20\textsuperscript{th} century both to evaluate different design solutions, and to achieve the highest possible coverage of the design space during the exploration process  \autocite{griesMethodsEvaluatingCovering2004}.

CubeSat projects have traditionally followed less formal approaches to design \todo{[cite]}. The \acs{NASA} \acs{SYE} handbook defines the following steps to defining a design solution \autocite[66]{sheaNASASystemsEngineering2020}:\footnote{Presented here simplified}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=-2ex]
	\item Define alternative design solutions
	\item Analyse each design solution
	\item Select the best alternative
	\item Verify the solution
	\item If needed, repeat this process for lowel-level products
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph[Concurrent Design]{\acf{CDE}}
Traditionally, the initial design phases of a space system are performed using a Concurrent Engineering or \emph{\acf{CDE}} method, where all subsystem experts are gathered in a single location to iterate over the design elements of the system. These iterations are aided by automated simplified analyses, usually performed with simple spreadsheet software and mission analysis tools \autocite{bandecchiConcurrentEngineeringApplied1999}.
The \acs{CDE} method is supported by specialised tools that allow almost instant exchange of parameters and data between experts,\footnote{This software typically has features that assist with the design solution defininition. For example, different \emph{options} of parameter sets can be created, and then \emph{evaluated} based on algorithmically calculated metrics, answering the two main questions of design definition.} along with technical support in a physical location that facilitates screen sharing and online collaboration \autocite{ranaVisionNextGenConcurrent2021}. This exchange can also take place through online meetings \autocite{retselisAdaptationAcubeSATNanosatellite2022}, although \citeauthor{bandecchiConcurrentEngineeringApplied1999} have noted the importance of organic interactions to influence the design rather than cooperation during strictly scheduled meetings only \cite{bandecchiConcurrentEngineeringApplied1999}. \todo{I don't know if this citation is the one where bandecchi mentions this, or if it has been written somewhere.} The \acs{CDE} method is often viewed as an alternative to a centralised \acs{SYE} authority, which decides on the high-level design and subsystem requirements, which then flow down to individual vendors.

Within the context of the \acs{SCS} methodology, the design solution definition process has to be repeated in the beginning of every iteration (\Cref{fig:m_sat_scrum_iteration}). On the other hand, the small teams dedicated to \acs{SCS} are usually co-located or are able to communicate on a frequent bases. These characteristics make it possible to \textbf{implement \acs{CDE} during every iteration of the project}. This idea makes it possible to evaluate the different dependencies between disciplines and provide instant feedback on design changes.

\begin{margindefinition}[Timebox]
	Timeboxing refers to the practice of strictly limiting the duration of an activity (e.g.~a meeting, an iteration, or a process) to a predefined length of time. It is especially useful when diminishing returns are expected as the activity becomes longer.
\end{margindefinition}
Depending on the project, the \acs{CDE} sessions can follow a formal, tool-based structure, or an informal approach, where information is stored in documents or directly insterted into domain-specific models, where available. An important aspect is to keep these sessions timeboxed, as the primary \draft{way} of generating information in Agile methodologies is through experimentation, and not up-front design. The resolution of the \acs{CDE} result can be better than a high-level subsystem specification, but needs not answer low-level questions that can be handled by engineers as part of their daily work.

\paragraph[On Big Design Up-Front]{On \acf{BDUF}}
Since common practices and the \acs{SCS} methodology separate design into more than one iteration, a question that often comes up is \emph{``how much time to spend on initial design''}.
The \acs{NASA} \acs{SYE} handbook notes how changing the design at a later point becomes harder or more costly, making the case for specifying an initial detailed design early in the project \cite{sheaNASASystemsEngineering2020}.
On the other hand, \citeauthor{watermanHowMuchUpFront2015} \cite{watermanHowMuchUpFront2015} claim that a trade-off between time spent in \acs{BDUF} needs to be made:
\begin{quote}
If an agile software team spends too little time designing architecture up-front then the team faces increased risk and higher chance of failure; if the team spends too much time the delivery of value to the customer is delayed, and responding to change can become extremely difficult \autocite{watermanHowMuchUpFront2015}.
\end{quote}
The authors note the following factors as reducing the need or effectiveness of a \acl{BDUF}:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=-3ex]
	\item Requirements instability
	\item Early value
	\item Team culture
	\item Customer agility
	\item Team experience
\end{itemize}
Conversely, they note that the existence of high technical risk and a need to reduce that risk reduce the effectiveness of iterative design. \todo{Maybe we can cite the software convex curve paper here?}

Therefore, the ideal decision on time spent for early design will vary based on the members of each time. However, typical \acs{SCS} environments, coupled with the emergent features of such systems, can generally afford a reduced early design time.

\subparagraph{Mathematical Model for design effort}

The drawbacks of incorrect allocation of design time early or late in the project are usually represent in terms of time and cost \begin{enumerate*}
	\item due to unnecessary design effort, or
	\item due to reworks because of errors uncovered late.
\end{enumerate*}
As the results of this trade-off are not selected beforehand, but are probabilistic in nature, a stochastic model can be created to model the design--verification--rework cycle.
This stochastic process for a single product iteration can be modelled as an absorbing Markov model (\Cref{fig:m_rework_markov}) \autocite{grinsteadChapter11Markov1998}.

\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\begin{tikzpicture}[shorten >=1pt,node distance=2.1cm,]
		\node (q_0)  [state,initial] {Design};
		\node (q_1)  [state,right=of q_0]   {Rework};
		\node (q_2)  [state,right=of q_1]   {End};
		
		\path[->] (q_0) edge[bend right=10] node[above,yshift=1mm] {$p_{1}$} (q_1)
		edge[bend left=35] node[above] {$1-p_{1}$} (q_2)
		(q_1) edge [loop below] node        {$p_{2}$} ()
		edge [bend right=20] node[above,yshift=2mm]       {$1-p_{2}$} (q_2)
		(q_2)
		edge [loop below] node        {1} ();
	\end{tikzpicture}
	\caption{Representation of a Markov process describing the design--rework cycle}
	\label{fig:m_rework_markov}
\end{figure}

% simplified mathematical model can be created to describe the design--verification--rework cycle, based on the possibility of defects unidentified during the design.
For this model, I am making the folowing assumptions:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=-1ex]
	\item A first transient state, \textbf{\emph{``Design''}}, represents the completion of the initial engineering effort to produce the iteration's \draft{product}.
	
	A second transient state, \textbf{\emph{``Rework''}}, represents the completion of an added iteration with additional engineering effort to design and produce an extra product, in case of defects or unfulfilled needs found during the previous iteration.
	
	The final absorbing state, \textbf{\emph{``End''}}, represents a product which has completed its iteration's goals.
	\item The \emph{``Design''} state implies that verification of the current iteration (through analysis or testing) has been completed. There is a probability \( p_1 \) that the iteration has not succeeded in its goals, and the model starts transitioning to the \textbf{\emph{``Rework''}} state.
	
	After the \emph{``Rework''} state, there is a probability that the open issues have still not been resolved, or that new ones appeared during the rework process. In this case, there is a probability \( p_2 \) that the system transitions again to the \emph{``Rework''} state. In the case where an iteration was added only to address the issues identified in the previous iteration, I expect \( p_2 < p_1 \).
	\item States and transitions are associated with a mathematical \emph{cost}. This cost can represent time spent, monetary cost, or an objective function that combines different resources as needed. Transitioning to a state incurs the following costs:
	\begin{itemize}[itemsep=-2ex]
		\item For the \textbf{\emph{``Design''}} state, the cost of the \emph{initial} design process \( C_{d0} \), the recurring manufacturing costs \( C_m \), and the cost of verification \( C_v \).
		\item For the \textbf{\emph{``Rework''}} state, the cost of the additional design \( C_d \), manufacturing \( C_m \) and \( C_v \).
	\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Given the above, the problem of measuring the expected cost of the Markov process is reduced to calculating \emph{the expected number of visits to the \textbf{\emph{``Rework''}}} state.

\marginnote{The canonical representation of a transition matrix of an absorbing Markov chain is given by \cite{grinsteadChapter11Markov1998} as:
\[
\mathbf P = \left[
	\begin{matrix}
		\mathbf{Q} & \mathbf{R} \\
		\mathbf{0} & \mathbf{I}_r
	\end{matrix}
\right]
\]
where each row represents a source state, each column a destination state, \( \mathbf{Q} \) a \( t \times t \) transition matrix and \( \mathbf{R} \) a \( t \times r \) transition matrix.

For an absorbing Markov chain, the \(i,j\) entry of the \emph{fundamental matrix} \( \mathbf{N} \) is the expected number of times the chain is in state \( s_j \) if it starts in state \( s_i \) \cite{grinsteadChapter11Markov1998}, where:
\[
\mathbf{N} = \left( \mathbf{I}_t - \mathbf{Q} \right)^{-1}
\]

For a \( 2 \times 2 \) matrix, the inverse can be simply calculated as:
\[
\mathbf{N}^{-1} = \frac{1}{N_{11}N_{22} - N_{12}N_{21}} \left[
	\begin{matrix}
		N_{22} & -N_{12} \\
		-N_{21} & N_{11}
	\end{matrix}
\right]
\]
}
The Markov chain of \Cref{fig:m_rework_markov} has \( t=2 \) transient states and \( r=1 \) absorbing state.
Then the transition matrix is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P} = \left[
	\begin{matrix}
		0 & p_1 & 1 - p_1 \\
		0 & p_2 & 1 - p_2 \\
		0 & 0   & 1
	\end{matrix}
	\right]
\end{equation}
The fundamental matrix is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
	%\nonumber
	\mathbf{N} = \left( \mathbf{I}_t - \mathbf{Q} \right)^{-1}
	%\nonumber
	= \left[
		\begin{matrix}
			1 & - p_1 \\
			0 & 1 - p_2
		\end{matrix}
	\right]^{-1}
	= \frac{1}{1 - p_2} \left[
		\begin{matrix}
			1 - p_2 & p_1 \\
			0       & 1
		\end{matrix}
		\right]
\end{equation}
Therefore, the expected number of visits to the \emph{``Rework''} state, starting from the \emph{``Design''} state, is:
\begin{equation}
	E_{\mathrm{rework}} = N_{12} = \frac{p_1}{1 - p_2}
\end{equation}

Considering the above assumptions, the total cost of the design--verification--rework cycle is:
\begin{equation}
	C = C_{d_0} + E_{\mathrm{rework}} C_d + \left( E_{\mathrm{rework}} + 1 \right) \left( C_m + C_v \right)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Modularity}

\todo{For sources of this section, check bibliography of mathieuAssessingFractionatedSpacecraft2006}

A defining characteristic of CubeSats and, to a lesser extent, PocketQubes, is their \textbf{modular structure}.

\begin{definition}[Modularity]
	Modularity can be understood as the use of interfaces to connect different subsystems/components of a system, in a way that allows them to be separated and recombined in different ways.
	\todo{[ref?]}
\end{definition}
Modularity can be described in terms of:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=-2ex]
	\item \textbf{Structural Modularity}, where the interfaces are physical, and
	\item \textbf{Electrical Modularity}, where the interfaces are electrical, and used to transfer power and/or data.
\end{itemize}

In CubeSats, modularity is achieved through the use of PC/104-sized boards, along with a roughly-defined electrical interconnect specification \autocite{BoardSpecificationLibreCube}. Commercial CubeSat subsystems can then be placed inside a shared subsystem ``stack'', and often rearranged as needed. A list of protocols, such as \acs{I2C} or \acs{CAN}, allow the communication between subsystems of different manufacturers.
However, in practice, different subsystems may show incompatibilities with each other. There is also no shared specification for low-level interfaces, meaning that additional work may be needed to allow different subsystems to communicate.

For \acs{SCS}, modularity presents more challenges, as it introduces constraints to a system's design. For example:
\begin{itemize}
	\item In a PC/104 board, the 104-pin connector covers \qtyproduct{6 x 0.8}{cm} of the \qtyproduct{9.6 x 9.0}{cm} board, taking up \qty{5.6}{\percent} of the available area, even though all 104 pins are rarely used. \todo{[ref?]}
	\item The \acs{CAN} bus used by some CubeSat subsystems requires two \qty{120}{\ohm} terminating resistors, which will consume a maximum of \qty{33.3}{\milli\watt} \footnote{Assuming a constant \(\Delta V = \qty{2}{\volt} \)} during operation.\footnote{This is generally negligible for CubeSats, but a significant amount for ChipSats with an \acs{OAP} of, for example, \( \sim \qty{100}{\milli\watt} \).}
	\item Most standard CubeSat packages separate the \emph{\acf{COMMS}} and \emph{\ac{OBDH}} functions into different boards, in order to accomodate the required flexibility of supporting different communication schemes and different subsystem vendors respectively. However, recent single-purpose missions have combined \ac{COMMS} and \ac{OBDH} into a single board, as \ac{OBDH} functionality is not usually resource-intensive.
\end{itemize}
While the above anecdotal examples do not need to translate to \acs{SCS} as-is, they show that the concept of modularity has to add additional constraints to a system. As \acs{SCS} designs often try to optimise available real estate, mass, power consumption and general performance, \textbf{a modular design may not be optimal in every situation.} This is especially valid in the case of femto- and atto-satellites, whose simple \acs{PCB} shape does not favour additional effort spent to make subsystems modular. I also note that modularity is not a binary decision, but can involve \draft{different decisions across different parts of the system}.

In the absence of representative hardware, simulators or mock-ups can be used, as long as they do not require disproportionate effort to implement. Remote or concurrent access to hardware is highly desirable as well. • Based on the project, different programming languages or techniques can be used, with the aim of increasing reliability without sacrificing development speed. Examples include static analysis, automated testing/fuzzing, Design by Contract (DbC), Domain-Specific Languages (DSLs), or automated code generation.  Finally, for both aspects, technology and code reuse is highly encouraged [85].}

\subsection{Interface Management Guidelines}
\label{sec:METHODOLOGY_interface_management}

\draft{REWRITE!!! External interfaces with the launch provider or other entities may require components not directly available to the development team. As such, they may not be available for immediate experimentation and testing within a few sprint, and require specific attention during development [79, p. 95]. Such interfaces and interface requirements can be categorised into:  • Mechanical (e.g allowable envelope, fasteners, load limits) • Electrical (e.g interconnects, voltage/power limits, deployment switches) • Software (e.g protocols, data rates, packet throughput)  As interface compliance is a “must-have” requirement for all missions, interfaces should be implemented early in the design and documented in greater detail than the rest of the system. Depending on the launch opportunity selected, hosts may agree on a specific interface with their guests, share detailed documentation, or provide “mock” interface simulators to them. Developers should validate interfaces as early as possible, and attempt to integrate prototype implementations with the host’s systems (“hardware-in-the-loop” testing [76]). This will allow them to follow the need for system-level verification at every iteration of our process. They should also be cautious of revisions, especially when the flight heritage of their host is limited. If possible, developers should be physically present to verify the final hand-off or integration before launch, and allow for a few days to correct small deviations if needed. In some cases, the criticality of such interfaces might be high enough to require proof that no system external to the satellite might be negatively impacted. Metrics such as the ECSS severity scale [86] can be used to classify this impact, ranging from negligible to catastrophic (such as loss of launcher or major injury) effects. For example, an International Space Station (ISS) launch may have specific limits on electrical system configuration, deployment switches and battery cell heritage. Interfaces with catastrophic failure modes should be verified formally and independently from the rest of the system, as risk cannot be tolerated in this case.}

\subsection{Verification \& Validation Guidelines}
\label{sec:METHODOLOGY_verification}

Verification and validation is an integral part of a sprint cycle, and is the stage that generates insight about the developed systems that can be reused as lessons learned and applied to the next cycle.

%The distinction between the ``verification'' and ``validation'' terms is provided 

\begin{margindefinition}[Validation]
	\textbf{Validation} is the process which demonstrates that the product is able to accomplish its intended use in the intended operational environment \cite{ECSS-S-ST-00-01C}.
\end{margindefinition}

In traditional space systems engineering, \acs{ECSS} defines verification as the process demonstrating that the built system satisfies all requirements and is free of defects \cite{ECSS-S-ST-00-01C}. However, in the \ac{SCS} methodology, the system is not strictly defined by its requirements specification, but also by an agreement between the developers and stakeholders on its capability and performance. Therefore, based on the \acs{ECSS} definition \cite{ECSS-S-ST-00-01C}, I \draft{modify the definition of verification as follows:}

\begin{definition}[Verification]
	\textbf{Verification} is the process which generates objective evidence to evaluate a product artifact, with the goals of:
	\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=-2ex]
		\item Assessing the number of requirements that the product is compliant with, based on the current requirements specification (see \Cref{sec:METHODOLOGY_requirements})
		\item Demonstrating that the product is free of defects
		\item Measuring the product's performance
		\item \draft{Confirming the implementation of all features requested as part of work packages}
	\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}


While verification ensures that the built system satisfies all agreed-on specifications and expectations, \textbf{validation} ensures that the correct system was built \autocite{ECSS-S-ST-00-01C}.

\begin{margindefinition}[Performance]
	\textbf{Performance} is the set of quantifiable characteristics of a system \emph{(adapted from \cite{ECSS-S-ST-00-01C})}.
\end{margindefinition}

\acs{ECSS} defines four verification methods that are typically used in space missions: \emph{review-of-design, analysis, inspection and testing}, which provide different levels of fidelity and reliability \autocite{ECSS-E-ST-10-02C}. It also distinguishes between \emph{acceptance} and \emph{qualification} levels for design parameters, the former of which are used to verify workmanship and functionality, and the latter used to demonstrate design margins, usually at a level that may be destructive for the \ac{DUT} \autocite{ECSS-E-ST-10-03C}.
Verification is said to be made in ``ambient'' conditions if no attempt is made to simulate the space environment,\footnote{Mainly temperature, pressure, radiation, and electromagnetic environment.} or are classified as ``environmental'' otherwise.

Verification is traditionally done during the dedicated Phase D. Launch providers usually require some form of analysis and testing, in order to ensure that any failures on the hosted systems do not propagate to the host or other clients. \marginnote{For example, SpaceX requires \draft{this kind of testing, cite spacex rideshare}}On the other hand, validation is typically said to be performed during the operational Phase E, where the usefulness and correctness of a system can be asserted after its actual use. When the V-model is used, verification can be performed in a tiered fashion, starting from subsystems to the integrated system \autocite{bundesrepublikdeutschlandVModellXT2006}. On the other hand, smaller systems like CubeSats regularly perform prototyping as part of their early phases, as part of risk-reduction or development activities \autocite{sousaCubeSatDevelopmentFramework2021,karvinenUsingHobbyPrototyping}, where functional or environmental verification takes place before a system-level verification phase.

In the context of \ac{SCS}, verification and validation present an worthwhile target for optimisation.
The low development cost of \ac{SCS} means that the cost and time overhead to create a \textbf{prototype} are low and can be part of the regular cycles of an Agile lifecycle process, which routinely include verification.
While CubeSats traditionally experiment with subsystem prototypes or reduced system mock-ups, in the case of \ac{SCS}, the \textbf{entire system} can be verified as part of these short cycles.

The most critical physical aspects of a design\footnote{Such as the verification of the functionality of deployables and actuators, the verification of masses and envelopes, thermal and electromagnetic behaviour \draft{etc.}} have traditionally involved both detailed analysis and testing for verification. In particular, environmental testing of subsystems and systems requires costly dedicated facilities; developers are therefore typically incentivised to create and execute domain-specific models and analyses of their systems before committing to a testing campaign.

The fidelity of these models and analyses can vary. For example, a power consumption analysis might simply creating a spreadsheet with the nominal consumption values of all components and an appropriate margin, or a detailed time-domain simulation of component behaviour during different mission phases, and how they affect electrical converter efficiency. Different fidelities can be also sought in thermal and structural analyses, where the spatial/temporal resolution can be configured and different models can be used for the representation of nodes and interfaces.

Similarly, testing processes can involve different types of equipment and fidelity. Functional testing can take place in an ambient environment, usually in a so-called ``FlatSat'' configuration, where all subsystems are laid out in a flat surface\marginnote{In the case that \aclp{EM} are not available for all subsystems, different methods can be used to perform a reduced, yet complete satellite functional test. \acl{HIL} rely on software components that test specific hardware, or alternatively hardware/software-based subsystem mocks can be used that emulate missing parts. Complete software emulation of external devices can be also used to verify functional requirements. \todo{citations}} and full functional or mission  tests are executed manually or (semi)automatically.
The required frequency of functional tests for complex mission means that it is often impossible to test the complete functionality of a system at once (especially in cases where \draft{payload runs} need multiple days to complete). Different test coverage models are therefore typically defined in missions.

	\item Full Functional Test
	\item ``Day-in-life`` Test

}


On the other hand, environmental verification usually involves specialised equipment\footnote{Such as shakers, shock benches, \ac{TVAC} chambers, or anechoic chambers} that is used to simulate space conditions for the \ac{DUT}.
The emulation of these conditions is necessary in order to examine their expected and unexpected effects on electronics, structures and mechanisms.\marginnote{These conditions can also affect component behaviour that is difficult or impractical to analyse, such as the behaviour of \acs{COTS} fastener materials, or temperature-dependent electronic performance degradation \todo{format}}

Past performance of these structures, especially in terms of rigidity and thermal behaviour, is well understood from previous missions. This reduces the benefits of analysis.

In general, the focus of software developers should be to achieve a balance between delegating functionality and complexity to the spacecraft and the ground segment. More specifically:
\begin{itemize}
	\item On the one hand, performing calculations and finely controlling the spacecraft from the ground makes it easier for operators to handle unexpected issues and improve functionality.
	\item On the other hand, on-the-edge processing is necessary to reduce the downlinked data in the case of restricted data budgets.
\end{itemize}

\newthought{One} important capability that should be planned from the beginning is \textbf{software reprogramming}, which allows developers to patch the on-board software even after launch. While it is recommended by most standards and guides, it is by far the most complex feature to implement, as it depends on the hardware capabilities of each processor, usually involves handling large data volumes, and requires knowledge of the fundamentals of the firmware architectures used.

A \emph{dual-bank architecture} can support in-orbit programming with relative ease and low risk, by trading off an amount of non-volatile memory.
In this architecture, program memory is split into two halves, one holding the working copy of the software, and another where the new version is progressively uploaded. This architecture provides a permanent space to upload and verify a new software version, and a safe way of performing a switch-over when the upload is complete. It is also supported natively by some microcontroller families.

In typical CubeSat systems engineering documentation, developers are recommended to devote 50\% of a project's time to testing activities \todo{[cite]}. Within the context of \acs{SCS}, testing takes place as part of normal development and during each iteration\todo{ [ref section]}. In the context of software engineering, verification and testing is performed in both an automated\footnote{referred to as computer-based testing} and manual fashion. \citeauthor{myersArtSoftwareTesting2012} separate software verification into the following categories \autocite{myersArtSoftwareTesting2012}:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Code Inspections, Walkthroughs \& Reviews}: This refers to a review of the static original source code. Code reviews are usually performed after every code change (``commit''), but can also take place independently (usually in the form of audits).  \citeauthor{myersArtSoftwareTesting2012} recommend different techniques for code reviews, which can involve different levels of scrutiny, conversation with the original authors and review checklists. While code reviews are not strictly part of software testing, a longer time spent on reviewing is correlated with an increase in code quality \autocite{chengWhatImprovesDeveloper2022, kemererImpactDesignCode2009}. Code inspection can also involve automated static analysis through automated tools.
	
	\item \textbf{Test-case execution}: The most common form of software testing involves the creation of programmatic test-cases that verify individual software modules or their integration. The goal of executing test-cases is usually two-fold:
	\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=-2ex]
		\item To verify compliance with a functional specification, and
		\item To ensure that as many as possible states of the software are covered.
	\end{enumerate}

	Since traditional Agile software engineering does not involve the detailed specification of functional requirements, \textbf{the test suite itself serves as the specification}. This is part of the philosophy of, e.g., \acf{TDD}, where the tests are created \emph{before} the actual software is implemented \autocite{beckTestDrivenDevelopment2002}. This approach is not dissimilar to formal model checking, where a model of a system is analysed to ensure that it meets a certain pre-defined specification \autocite{berardSystemsSoftwareVerification2001}. However, model checking requires creating an additional model of the system, which needs to be further kept correct and requires symbolic manipulation to avoid the state explosion \cite{berardSystemsSoftwareVerification2001}. 

	As such, in practice, developers create a limited number of test-cases that aim to evaluate the most likely and representative states of the software, with the goal of increasing statement and branch coverage of the source code. Additional techniques used to cover potentially invalid states include edge-case testing and regression testing. For complex software, test-cases evaluate code modules at low (unit) or high (integration) levels \autocite{myersArtSoftwareTesting2012}.

	Nevertheless, various approaches are often used in academia and industry to increase software reliability through different methods of formal verification, with examples being Ada's SPARK \todo{[cite]}, Frama-C for C \autocite{kirchnerFramaCSoftwareAnalysis2015} and \draft{Prusti} for Rust \autocite{astrauskasLeveragingRustTypes2019}. However, the complexity and effort required with applying these methods prevents their direct application to entire space systems \autocite{jacklinSurveyVerificationValidation2015}.

	\item \textbf{User Testing}: Usually referred to as ``usability testing'', this involves the actual use and interaction with software by its intended users. Even though \acs{SCS} software systems lack a user interface\footnote{Although one could still evaluate user-facing properties of the software, such as error message fidelity, response time, degree of available functionality etc.}, user testing expresses a need to perform a manual evaluation of the software running on actual hardware after every iteration, to make sure that the \draft{integration with untested systems is valid.}
\end{enumerate}

Additional testing methods that increase confidence in the design include:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Fuzz Testing} (or fuzzing) involves the generation of a vast dataset of random test cases as inputs, which are then fed to the software to evaluate any missed edge-cases\todo{ [cite]}.
	\item \textbf{Stress Testing} evaluates the performance of the software when it is put under a heavier load\footnote{``Load'' in this case can refer to data volume, data rate, concurrent connections, number of operations etc.} than expected\todo{ [cite]}.
	\item \textbf{Model Checking}: \draft{[todo]} \todo{[todo]}
\end{enumerate}

While the benefits of testing are well understood, software quality assurance has historically \draft{experienced} a lack of adequate time and resource allocation, combined with a potential lack of motivation for testing engineers \autocite{hardmanTestingNotBoring2025}. This situtation becomes exacerbated in embedded systems development, since the verification of embedded software systems requires some form of hardware access as well.

\newthought{Therefore}, the recommendations for software testing within the context of \acs{SCS} entail finding an optimal point between effort spent testing and reliability improvements. Indicatively, they can involve:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Implementing an embedded testing suite} that lets developers execute and evaluate microcontroller code. This can involve, for example:
	\begin{itemize}[itemsep=-1ex]
		\item A \textbf{\acf{HIL}} system, where all the relevant hardware components\footnote{Development Boards, Engineering Models, or Qualification/Flight Models} are physically tested in tandem. Specifically for implementors of the \acs{SCS} methodology, the existence of a complete system model for every iteration \draft{makes this approach easier}.\marginnote{A practical \acs{HIL} setup can involve, for example, an ``instrumentation'' computer that sends \acs{TC} to the \acl{OBC} of the satellite, and evaluates the \acs{TM} received, as part of high-level test cases. Low-level test cases can be implemented by uploading custom testing software to the microcontroller, or by including a test-suite in development versions of the software.}
		\item A simulated version of the satellite, running completely on a desktop computer, and based on microcontroller and peripheral emulation.
	\end{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Implementing static analysis tools}, which can assist the code review process by identifying non-compliances to accepted best practices. Many static analysis tools can be used as ``drop-in'' \draft{plugins to standard compliation pipelines.}
	\item \textbf{Attempting to reach maximum test coverage}, as coverage is one of the metrics typically correlated with defect rate.\marginnote{While coverage can be simply evaluated in plain \acs{SW} testing, embedded systems are not instrumented to share this information by default. To that end, \citeauthor{tsiodrasCoverMe2016} provides a hardware-agnostic coverage generation method based on the gdb debugger \autocite{tsiodrasCoverMe2016}.}
	\item \textbf{Automating the testing process}: The functional requirements of \acs{SCS} usually involve simple \draft{instructions}, and most can be evaluated by plain protocols and processes, such as nominal \acs{TMTC}. This makes \acs{SCS} functional testing suitable for \acf{RPA} methods, where data transfer, analysis and event 
	trigerring are performed by an automated process \autocite{hofmannRoboticProcessAutomation2020}. The availability of ready-to-use automation tools reduces the developer effort needed to automate this process \draft{as well}.
	\item \textbf{Focus on integration testing before unit testing}, as most errors are usually observed on the boundaries of components and the interactions between them \todo{[cite]}. This includes a special focus on \textbf{external interfaces} that are attached to a spacecraft.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Programming Languages}

An increasing number of programming languages are starting to be used for small space system firmware, which have started moving away from the compiled, statically-type paradigm. Languages such as Python \autocite{hollidayPyCubedOpenSourceRadiationTested2019}, Java \todo{[cite]} and shell scripts\todo{ [cite]} have been used for CubeSats, along with the compiled options of C, C++, Ada and Rust.
While the aforementioned research notes a weak correlation between programming language and software reliability in typical \acs{SW} engineering contexts, typical \acs{SCS} projects differ in terms of team size, developer experience, and fault tolerance\todo{  [cite]}. This difference can potentially increase the effect of programming language selection on fault prevention.\marginnote{\todo{What is a fault?}}

\citeauthor{ashmoreProgrammingLanguageEvaluation2022} have hypothesised that specific programming language features can support the detection or prevent the introduction of errors, providing the example of the \emph{Rust} language which avoids memory allocation errors by design. \draft{\citeauthor{rayLargescaleStudyProgramming2017} \autocite{rayLargescaleStudyProgramming2017} analysed 728 GitHub projects, identifying commits that fixed bugs, and concluded that ``there is a small but significant relationship between language class and defects''.
\citeauthor{nanzComparativeStudyProgramming2015} \autocite{nanzComparativeStudyProgramming2015} measured runtime failures in programs of the \emph{Rosetta Code} repository, finding that ``compiled strongly-typed languages are significantly less prone to runtime failures than interpreted or weakly-typed languages, since more errors are caught at compile time''.}

Both CubeSat and \acs{SCS} software development team are usually faced with a choice of programming language when starting development. Based on the above \draft{findings, it would be an interesting research question to evaluate if a specific programming language or selection criteria can have a measurable effect on the final software reliability}. As many members of the CubeSat community follow an open-source philosophy, there is a multitude of space software projects available online, that can be used as case-studies to investigate the effect of language on software defect rate.

More specifically, the execution and testing plan is elaborated into the following steps (\Cref{fig:m_software_fuzz_activity}):
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Static Analysis}: Involves extracting common metrics to identify code size (\acs{SLOC}) and complexity (cyclomatic complexity) for each tested framework, in order to acquire a general idea about the size \draft{?} of the codebase. I generated this information using the Lizard static analysis tool Lizard analyser \autocite{yinLizardExtensibleCyclomatic2019}\footnote{More specifically, I used a modified version of Lizard, available at \url{https://github.com/kongr45gpen/lizard}, to provide additional and normalised metrics.}
	\item \textbf{Emulation and Execution}: Each framework is designed for execution on different sets of hardware, microcontrollers and architectures. However, to ensure similar testing conditions, it would be ideal to employ a similar hardware platform for all tested frameworks, as it is only software that is being evaluated. At the same time, testing on software only, without any hardware involvement, provides much better observability and controllability for the framework under test.
	
	Therefore, I decided to use the \textbf{Qemu Emulator}. Qemu provided a common machine to emulate all frameworks\footnote{More specifically, \texttt{mps2-an500} with a Cortex-M7 core for real-time frameworks, and \texttt{virt} with a Cortex-A9 core for Linux guests. I used Yocto Linux to create appropriate distributions for emulation.}. Each framework had to also be adapted for the relevant Qemu machine, by removing machine-specific code from the repository, and inserting generic Qemu-compatible code.\footnote{To ensure repeatability, all patches applied to the software are treated as ``treatments'', are tracked and versioned.}

	To provide \ac{IO} to the machines, I connected a custom-built device to the emulator, mocking the communication protocols\footnote{Responsible for \ac{TMTC}, for example via \acs{UART} or a custom datagram-based protocol}, sensors (e.g.~temperature) and an error handling observer.
	\item \textbf{Unit Testing}: To verify the correctness of the framework, the emulator and the communication protocol implementation, I used a common set of unit tests to verify the basic functional requirements, mainly involving \acs{TC} execution and \acs{TM} reception. The results of these functional tests are shown in \Cref{tab:m_software_fuzz_functional}.
	\item \textbf{Fuzz Testing}:
	The main part of this evaluation involved performing a dynamic evaluation of the frameworks under test. Fuzzing was selected as the method most likely to identify missed faults and edge cases that still exist on the actual source code.

	I used the AFL++ (American Fuzzy Loop) fuzzer for this evaluation \autocite{fioraldiAFLCombiningIncremental2020}, as it is a grey-box fuzzer with configurable mutators and native support for execution under the Qemu emulator, and more specifically used the LibAFL framework\footnote{LibAFL is itself written in Rust and provides a fork of Qemu with an augmented \acs{API} to support fuzzing execution} \autocite{fioraldiLibAFLFrameworkBuild2022}.

	The fuzzing process involved:
	\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=-2ex]
		\item Generating a corpus, based on the valid \acs{TC} packets created for unit testing,
		\item Running the \draft{\emph{havoc} and \emph{Grimoire} mutators} in AFL to generate new permutations of the inputs,
		\item Feeding these inputs to the framework under test via the emulated communication interface in Qemu,
		\item Evaluating whether the software is in a good state, by checking for processor exceptions, Qemu exceptions, language panics, and by running a unit test (\acs{RFT}) suite, and
		\item Letting the fuzzer run for \draft{24 hours} to generate a \draft{sizeable} dataset.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}

\subparagraph{Conclusions}

Based on the above, \todo{blablabla}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The selection of programming language alone \textbf{cannot guarantee program correctness}. \draft{Simple issues were identified in projects of all 4 evaluated languages.}
	Programming languages such as Rust, Ada and Python offer a default process for error handling, where unhandled exceptions lead to program termination. For example, Ada's \texttt{Unchecked\_Conversion} and Rust's \texttt{unwrap} calls can lead to a panic if the state of their input is not well established and reasoned about. Creating a program where such ``unsafe'' calls are avoided or properly handled requires a basic understanding of a language's error handling philosophy, and, where applicable, appropriate static checks.
	\item In C/C++, unsafe calls (such as null dereferencing or out-of-bounds array access) commonly lead to \textbf{undefined behaviour}, which can propagate as a crash (segmentation/bus fault), incorrect data, incorrect functionality, or an infinite loop. On the contrary, the default data access functions of Rust, Ada and Python perform run-time checks and guarantee either a handled panic on invalid calls, or the propagation of the error to a user-defined mechanism. 
	\item The Rust compiler enforces an \textbf{exhaustiveness check} on enumerations and function results by default. Although this can be bypassed, it serves as an additiona layer of safety against unhandled edge cases and logical paths.
	\item The reliability benefit of a programming language selection has to be considered in relation to the \textbf{effort} associated with writing code in that language, and the various other environmental factors affected by this choice.
	
	The frameworks tested did not present noteworthy differences in terms of size and complexity that are causally linked to the language. However, \acs{SCS} teams have to consider the knowledge/experience of developers for each language, along with its potential learning curve, as well as the size of the available ecosystem. An indication of this ``popularity'' is given in \Cref{tab:m_software_popularity}, which shows that Python and C/C++ have a larger ecosystem for embedded space applications compared to Rust, while Ada \draft{enjoys more than an order of magnitude more limited ecosystem.} In turn, this metric provides an idea on the availability of open-source libraries for reuse, as well as the common \draft{experience} of students and young graduates, who are more likely to participate in academic \acs{SCS} projects.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Communication Stack}
Many of \Cref{tab:m_software_requirements}'s requirements embody the communication of data between space and ground. Wireless transmission of data is complex by nature. Typically, this complexity is managed by separating the communication process into different layers, starting from the physical waves to the virtual application. \acs{ECSS} summarises the protocols used in space for each layer in the \emph{ECSS-E-ST-50C} standard \autocite{ECSS-E-ST-50C}. This includes a large variety of purpose-built protocols by the \acf{CCSDS}. As these protocols are optimised for use in space, they are typically not implemented in generally available or commercial software and hardware.
\marginnote{%
	For example, Manchester used an AX.25 packet format with a simple modulation for the \emph{KickSat} CubeSat, and a custom packet under \ac{DSSS} encoded with Gold Codes.%
}
CubeSat developers have therefore traditionally relied on custom (in-house) or generic solutions for communication, especially for the physical layer.

The selection of a protocol stack limits the compatible hardware and data budget of a spacecraft. In the case of \acs{SCS}, system characteristics further limit the available transmission power and effective antenna apertures, \draft{making devices look more like \ac{IOT} devices than spacecraft}.
The performance of an \acs{SCS} communication system is physically limited by the Shannon--Hartley theorem\todo{ [cite]}, and supported by concepts such as Spread Spectrum and Error-Correcting Codes, which help in trading off data rate for robustness to low receiver \acs{SNR}.

I provide a list of communication protocols in \Cref{tab:m_comms_protocols}. Proprietary protocols may be used instead when supported by a specific \acs{IC}, such as CC430 or nRF24. Reception of telemetry can be furter derisked by participating in a ground station network, such as SatNOGS or TinyGS\footnote{for LoRa only}.

\paragraph{Debugging Interfaces}
\label{sec:METHODOLOGY_debugging_interfaces}

One part of the hardware-software interface that is used often during development is the \textbf{programming} or \textbf{debugging interface}, which allows developers to:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=-2ex]
	\item Upload new software versions to a microcontroller
	\item Use a debugger to examine and alter the current state of the software
	\item Receive logs and \acs{TM} in various formats
	\item Inject \acl{TC}
\end{itemize}

This function is usually performed by a native debugging interface provided by the microcontroller\footnote{such as \acs{SWD} or \acs{JTAG}}, which is exposed through a dedicated \acs{PCB} connector. This interface then connects to a dedicated ``programmer'' device that interfaces between this \acs{MCU} signal and the programming computer. Spacecraft with multiple subsystems provide this functionality through a so-called \textbf{``umbilical connection''}, which provides diagnostic access to the satellite's power supply, batteries, and debugging interfaces.

\begin{marginfigure}
	\includegraphics{pictures/P1000717_resized.jpg}
	\caption{POQUITO's 16-pin umbilical connector, shown here above the antenna}
\end{marginfigure}

In CubeSats and PocketQubes, this interface is typically provided by a friction-locked connector, exposing each connection as a separate pin or plug. However, the limited space of femto-scale satellites motivates connection solutions that take up less space, by trading off compatibility with common connectors. Some notable examples are:
\marginnote{Typical considerations that have to be taken into account when selecting a physical umbilical interface are:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=-0.5ex]
	\item Physical size
	\item Number of distinct connections
	\item Maximum data rate
	\item Maximum current capacity
	\item Lock type
	\item Maximum number of mating cycles
\end{itemize}
}%
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{\acf{FFC} connectors and cables} that increase the density of connections, but usually require dedicated breakout boards to interface with \acs{COTS} \acs{EGSE}.
	\item \textbf{\acs{USB} connectors}, usually compliant to the USB-C specification, take advantage of the miniaturisation and power delivery capabilities of this common standard and allow directly interfacing a computer with the spacecraft. However, they require compatibility with the \acs{MCU}, additional power management hardware and a firmware bootloader. This approach is used by PyCubed-Mini \todo{[cite]}.
	\item \textbf{Other miniaturised connectors} that provide a high density, including industrial and commercial components. For example, the Monarch ChipSat used a 19-pin Micro-HDMI connector with a non-compliant pinout (\Cref{subfig:m_connectors_microhdmi}) \autocite{adamsRSelectedSpacecraft2019}.
	\item \textbf{Unpopulated vias, holes (``test points'') or pads} that can be accessed by probes, by soldering temporary connectors, or by using spring-loaded test pins. This approach is used by the Sprite (\Cref{subfig:m_connectors_pth}) \autocite{manchesterCentimeterScaleSpacecraftDesign2015} and SpinorSat \autocite{brashearsSpinorSatStep502018} ChipSats. To further reduce space, these nodes can be placed on \acs{PCB} edges. This kind of breakout pins were also envisioned by \citeauthor{adamsRSelectedSpacecraft2019}' StarChip concept \autocite{adamsRSelectedSpacecraft2019}. \todo{[check]}
	\item A \textbf{snap-off development board that encloses the ChipSat} and provides development-only interfaces. This is also a concept used by the Sprite ChipSats (\Cref{subfig:m_connectors_detachable}) \autocite{manchesterCentimeterScaleSpacecraftDesign2015}. The disadvantage is that, after the development board is separated, it is harder to reprogram the spacecraft (e.g.\space after integration).
	\item \textbf{\acf{OTA} programming through a wireless network}. This network can be the nominal \acs{RF} link used by the satellite, or a purpose-specific link, if supported by the microcontroller, such as Wi-Fi or Bluetooth. % NFC?
	\item \textbf{Programming through another wireless technology}, such as infrared/visible light or sound. This requires dedicated hardware.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Use of generative \acs{AI}}
\footnotetext{Credit: \href{https://dl.espressif.com/dl/schematics/pictures/esp32-wroom-32-front-back.jpg}{Espressif}}
Since the mid-2020s, \acs{AI}-assisted software development has become prevalent, through the use of \acfp{LLM} trained on publically available source code repositories.
\acs{AI} tools\footnote{Running on the cloud or locally} can offer assistance ranging from code completion to generation of code structures and architectures. The training set of coding models usually contains embedded firmware for popular platforms, \draft{making them compatible with typical \acs{SCS} software development.}

Initially, the use of generative \acs{AI} seems attractive for \acs{SCS} software, as it can easily generate large amounts of base code with less effort from developers. However, the quality and safety of generated code for use in mission-critical systems is still under investigation in research. A \citeyear{liuEmpiricalStudyCode2024} empirical study on three software problems found that ``the current language understanding and generation capabilities of the LLMs are not sufficient to generate complete functional code from highly specialized domain requirements'' \autocite{liuEmpiricalStudyCode2024}. The researchers found that, by default, the generated code from the specification may be incomplete and non-compliant to safety standards\footnote{In this case, the MISRA C standard.}. However, they noted that augmented prompts following a defined procedure can improve code quality.\marginnote{In \cite{liuEmpiricalStudyCode2024}, \citeauthor{liuEmpiricalStudyCode2024} compare \acs{LLM} code generation with model-based code generation, concluding that the latter requires a large investment of developer time and requires a correct model a priori. It therefore does not \draft{seem initially suitable for the \acs{SCS} methodology context.}}

In \citeyear{tihanyiHowSecureAIgenerated2025}, \citeauthor{tihanyiHowSecureAIgenerated2025} compared state-of-the-art \acsp{LLM} and found that 62\% of the generated C programs included at least one security vulnerability, such as buffer overflows and dereference failures \autocite{tihanyiHowSecureAIgenerated2025}.

On the other hand, a \citeyear{kirchnerGeneratingAutomotiveCode2025} comparison of locally-deployable \acsp{LLM} used an iterative generation (or ``code refinement'') process, where static and dynamic analysis is performed on the generated code, and the (potentially negative) results are fed back to the \acs{LLM}, until the acceptable code is produced \autocite{kirchnerGeneratingAutomotiveCode2025}. The researchers found that this method can produce code that performs the intended functionality and is compliant with the \draft{relevant} specifications.

\acsp{LLM} are constantly evolving as of 2026, and it is difficult to provide \draft{lasting} recommendations for their use in \acs{SCS} software development. \draft{Nevertheless, it seems that \acs{AI}-assisted coding can provide significant utility, as long as developers maintain an understanding of the principles and functions of the generated software, are aware of the potential safety and reliability risks, and perform adequate verification.}

In any case, teams should consider adherence to coding standards and best practices when developing. This can be aided by freely available static analysis tools, such as \emph{Cppcheck}, \emph{clang-analyzer} or \emph{NASA IKOS}. Various coding standards\footnote{such as MISRA or the JSF AV C++ standards} are used in the safety-critical software industry, but are often criticised due to their false positive rates, difficulty of static verification, and added ``noise'' \autocite{boogerdAssessingValueCoding2008}. A \citeyear{boogerdAssessingValueCoding2008} empirical study assessed the number of rule violations and actual faults of an industrial project overtime, finding a negative correlation \autocite{boogerdAssessingValueCoding2008}. Instead, they identified a set of 12 rules\footnote{Due to licensing restrictions of the MISRA standard, I cannot reproduce these rules here.} that performed well in predicting faults, commenting however that it is ``possible that adherence to the MISRA standard as a whole would have made the software less reliable''. In practical terms, static analysis tools provide recommendations of rule subsets that can be provide the required \draft{usability to developers}.

\section{ChipSat-1}
%Seminal papers: \autocite{borgueDevelopingDistributedFractionated2022a}

\todo{Need to check and ensure consistency for tenses}

ChipSat-1 was the first mission I participated in within the scope of this thesis, with the objective of generating data for femto-satellite projects and experimenting on an early iteration of the \ac{SCS} methodology (\Cref{chap:METHODOLOGY}).

The ChipSat-1 mission was motivated and constrained by a launch opportunity provided by a third-party satellite integrator, who offered to mount a ChipSat device as a secondary payload on one of their missions. The ChipSat itself was a technology demonstrator, intending to experiment with the use of \acf{VLC} between components of a distributed system. It consisted of a single \ac{PCB} separated into 3 regions (each emulating one part of a distributed system), measuring \qtyproduct{5 x 5}{\centi\meter} and weighing \SI{18.3}{\g} in total. Power generation and communication were handled by internal solar cells and a transceiver/antenna respectively, with the host providing only a mechanical and electrical inhibition interface.

This \emph{hosted payload} mission was developed in 2022, and environmentally tested and integrated in 2023. However, due to administrative issues with the host's launch provider, ChipSat-1's host satellite, and by extension, ChipSat-1, were never launched.

ChipSat-1 cost \SI{8930}{EUR} in material and testing costs, required a development time of roughly 10 months, and involved seven members of the team who contributed in different degrees. It was developed iteratively, with multiple representative hardware models having been built in the University of Luxembourg's CubeSat Lab. I participated as a Systems, Hardware and Software engineer in the project.

The objective of a \ac{VLC} demonstrator aligns with the prevalence and research in optical communication for ground-space and inter-satellite links, usually with lasers acting as transmitters \autocite{kaushalOpticalCommunicationSpace2017,khalighiSurveyFreeSpace2014,griffithsHighsensitivityIntersatelliteOptical2021}. Optical communication can offer situational advantages over traditional \ac{RF} links, especially in terms of directivity, bandwidth and power consumption. This comes at a cost of complexity, as laser transmitters and photodiode receivers need to be accurately managed and steered to achieve a reliable link. The use of \acfp{LED} instead of laser emitters trades off this high level of complexity at the cost of directivity. A simple single-\acs{LED} system performing \acf{OOK} can require a less complex implementation than an \ac{RF} transmitter.

\citeauthor{griffithsHighsensitivityIntersatelliteOptical2021} \autocite{griffithsHighsensitivityIntersatelliteOptical2021} have considered the use of \acp{SPAD} in combination with miniature \acp{LED} for an inter-satellite link, and performed a range and data rate simulation for different transmitter lenses. They found that a lens-less system, transmitting with \SI{1}{\milli\watt}, can achieve a \SI{2.87}{\kilo\meter} range at \SI{10}{\kilo\bit/\second}, which can be extended to \SI{141}{\kilo\meter} with a \( \sim \)\SI{2}{\degree} \acf{HPBW} lens.

\acsp{LED} and photodiodes as miniaturised \acs{COTS} components present an attractive use-case for \ac{SCS}, as the cost of reduced functionality that comes with small size can usually be accepted for such systems. The kilometer-scale ranges of \acs{LED}-based systems are for example applicable to satellite swarms that require networking \autocite{timmonsRangeBasedRelativeNavigation2022}. Compared to their \ac{RF} counterparts, these \ac{VLC} systems can be preferable when low power, high bandwidth and high directivity\footnote{For example, \citeauthor{griffithsHighsensitivityIntersatelliteOptical2021} \autocite{griffithsHighsensitivityIntersatelliteOptical2021} calculate that a \SI{2.5}{\centi\meter} diameter ACL25416 lens provides an equivalent of \SI{52.0}{\decibel i} of gain. However, such a directional system will require appropriate steering of the light source. On a centimetre-scale spacecraft this can be achieved, for example, using miniaturised mirrors steered by piezo-electric motors. \qtyproduct{1 x 1 x 0.5}{\centi\meter}-sized steering modules with \SI{0.1}{\deg} are commercially available \autocite{M3MicroBeamSteeringTechBulletin}.} is required, eliminating the need for a high-gain antenna or high-power transceiver.

ChipSat-1 was therefore decided to act as a technology demonstration experiment for a simple \ac{LED}-based \ac{VLC} system. Due to the limitation of a single flown spacecraft, we decided to place the transmitter (\ac{LED}) and receiver (photodiode) on a single \ac{PCB}, intending to focus on testing only the effects of ambient light, temperature and radiation on the payload and bus components.

In order to create a more representative example and emulate clock drift between the transmitter and the receiver, we split ChipSat-1 into two parts:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The \textbf{``primary''} part, transmitting data to Earth and receiving data from the secondary using photodiodes.
	\item The \textbf{``secondary''} part, generating data through environmental sensors and broadcasting it using an \acs{LED}, but without capability for Earth communication.
\end{itemize}

This configuration essentially emulates a \textbf{``fractionated''} system, where different parts of the intended functionality are separated into smaller independent spacecraft, instead of a larger monolithic one. This concept has been explored before for entire buses \autocite{mathieuAssessingFractionatedSpacecraft2006} or payloads \autocite{alandihallajMultiplepayloadFractionatedSpacecraft2022}.

For ChipSat-1, the available space allowed us to implement two ``secondary'' parts, designed to generate data that would be downlinked through the \ac{VLC} link: One had a gyroscope to measure and report angular velocity, and the other an ambient light intensity sensor.

Data is modulated by turning the \acsp{LED} on and off (\acs{OOK} --- \acl{OOK}) to transmit bytes, each bit corresponding to one symbol, with Manchester code applied\footnote{\emph{Manchester code} describes a pattern where one bit is encoded as low, then high, or high, then low. This guarantees that every symbol will have at least one bit transition. While this code reduces bit rate to half, it \begin{enumerate*}
		\item guarantees that no \ac{DC} component remains in the output signal, allowing easy high pass filtering to reduce the influence of ambient light (mainly sunlight);

A maximum target power generation budget can be approximated as follows \cite{SMAD}:

This theoretical analysis shows the expected power input, which should however be complemented with the relevant component selection. Most commercially available solar cells for space use follow a \qtyproduct{7 x 4}{\centi\meter} footprint.

Due to the high number of different commercially available solar cell sizes, I developed a Python optimisation tool based on rectangle-packing to identify the combination of solar cells that could theoretically produce the highest power.

\paragraph{Energy Storage}

While secondary\footnote{i.e.~rechargeable \autocite{SMAD}} batteries are the standard choice for energy storage in miniaturised spacecraft, the typical temperature conditions experienced by ChipSats are not suited for use of standard battery chemistries. More specifically, the thin and flat shape of ChipSats combined with their small thermal mass and lack of structure means that they lose their temperature very quickly during eclipse, being able to reach even  \SI{-100}{\degreeCelsius} \autocite{manchesterCentimeterScaleSpacecraftDesign2015}.

As most commercially available batteries do not support charging or discharging on temperatures significantly below \SI{0}{\degreeCelsius}, ChipSats typically forego long-term energy storage \cite{manchesterCentimeterScaleSpacecraftDesign2015}. While alternative solutions can be investigated, such as high-range \acs{LTO} batteries, coin-cell batteries, or heaters, they are associated with further drawbacks, reducing possible power draw or increasing size. ChipSat-1 was therefore decided to operate only with sunlight available.

\marginnote{At \qty{4}{\volt}, the capacitor can support a \qty{300}{\milli\watt} load for
	\( \qty{1}{\milli\farad} \cdot (\qty{4}{\volt})^2 / \qty{300}{\milli\watt} = \qty{53}{\milli\second} \).}
Nevertheless, each ChipSat-1 part includes a capacitor to provide short-term energy storage and eliminate energy fluctuations, especially in the case of high-power loads. We avoided the use of electrolytic capacitors due to safety concerns for their use in space \todo{ref}, using \qty{1}{\milli\farad} tantalum capacitors instead.

\subsection{Lessons Learned}

\draft{From a technical point of view, the following points were made clear during development of the spacecraft:}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=-1ex]
	\item Early versions and renders of the \acs{PCB} were available early in the project. These were useful as they could represent realistic concept art, used in communication with external partners and collaborators of our group.
	\item As the inhibit mechanism was managing a critical electrical interface, it should have been present in the early prototypes and iterations. At least a breadboard model should have been built for the inhibition circuit.
	\item Due to the small number of engineers participating in the project and their parallel involvement in projects separate to ChipSat-1, it was difficult to perform repeated and accurate design reviews, which would have detected some of the \draft{errors} before manufacturing. Personnel allocation and work package management are therefore crucial parts of a project like this. Even though a Gantt chart was created and updated monthly, project planning updates should have been more regular and the allocation of tasks clearer, with updates to the expected and completed amount of work for each task.
	\item While external manufacturing is convenient and results in high workmanship quality, it suffers from high lead times and difficulty of rework. In many cases, especially when design errors are expected, \ac{PCB} rework will be required in any case.
	In the case of ChipSat-1, a lead time of approx.~1 week was required for unpopulated \acs{PCB} procurement, and approx.~1 month for manufacturing. This was often augmented by unpredictable turnaround times for university procurement approval.
	Therefore, teams should consider investing and gaining knowledge regarding in-house manufacturing, especially in electronics and testing equipment.

\section{AI4Space}
Seminal papers: \autocite{thoemelLeanDemonstrationBoard2024} \autocite{thoemelAI4SpaceSnTLaunches}

The AI4Space mission was developed in the University of Luxembourg in the form of a payload to be hosted by a Skykraft Ltd.~micro-satellite.

\subsection{Mission Description}

AI4Space was hosted as part of Skykraft's payload hosting programme, mainly aimed at educational institutions \draft{reference archived website (?)}. More specifically, Skykraft provided slots accepting \qtyproduct{10 x 10}{\centi\meter} \acsp{PCB} (or ``Skykards''). These Skykards were electrically connected to their host with a \acs{PCI}-like edge connector, responsible for receiving energy and transferring data bidirectionally, through a proprietary serial interface. The requirements and interface details were communicated to users by the host company and updated at regular times.

The selection of a mission for the AI4Space platform was largely limited by the constraints of the payload launch opportunity provided. AI4Space was therefore largely a \emph{constraint-based} mission \draft{Reference}, restricted primarily by available size, weight, energy, and data budget.

The selected mission involved the detection and classification of \textbf{thermal events} using non-conventional sensors, combined with Machine Learning performed in orbit (``on the edge'').
More specifically, AI4Space intended to demonstrate the use of infrared thermal cameras to generate temperature information about a spacecraft's subsystems and components. This information would then be fed into a \ac{ML} algorithm to classify whether a component exhibits non-nominal (or anomalous) behaviour\footnote{By exhibiting, for example, an out-of-range temperature, or another erratic pattern}. As this is a simple classification problem, it is greatly supported by established \acs{ML} methods that can reasonably adapt to it.

AI4Space was therefore developed as a technology demonstrator platform for \acs{AI}-based anomaly detection, as well as an educational tool for payload and system development approaches.


\subsection{System Description and Justification}

\begin{figure}[h]
	\acuse{DC}
	\includegraphics{media/graphics/Block_AI4Space}
	\caption{Internal block diagram of the AI4Space system}
	\label{fig:c_ai4space_block}
\end{figure}


The functional part of the AI4Space payload, responsible for the generation of representative thermal images, consisted of:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=-2ex]
	\item Four thermal cameras looking towards a fixed point
	\item Four switchable resistors to emulate ``hot points'' 
	\item A dummy microcontroller\footnote{For easy integration, an ATMega Arduino processor was used.} to simulate a representative temperature increase during normal satellite operation
\end{enumerate}

The AI4Space payload contained no Attitude Control, Communications, or Electrical subsystems, as these aspects were covered in their entirety by the host spacecraft.

All other functions were covered by a \emph{Raspberry Pi Zero 2W}, serving as the system's \acf{OBC} and Payload Data Processor. The Raspberry Pi was chosen as a ready-to-use \acs{COTS} platform that essentially acts as a complete \acs{OBC}, and reduces the complexity of hardware design for a microcontroller. An additional benefit was the higher available set of programming languages or tools, seen as positive within the context of multiple software developers for the project. 

The incorporation of a Raspberry Pi implies the implementation of a complete base Linux system. This is usually a disadvantage in pocket-sized spacecraft due to the absence of real-time software and the higher energy consumption. However, \draft{given AI4Space's status as a payload and its high available energy}, these disadvantages were \draft{rejected} in favour of the additional convenience, tools, libraries and functions available in a Linux environment, especially considering the need for multiple software developers to be involved in the project. In fact, while most CubeSat projects rely on low-footprint \acp{MCU} for processing needs, Linux boards have found their place in some payload units \draft{Reference, e.g.~UpSat}.

\paragraph{Software}
AI4Space's software was divided into \textbf{microservices} \draft{ref microservice architecture}, each responsible for one part of the payload's operation or mission. These microservices were written in Python and \draft{connected} via the RabbitMQ message broker.

The use of a Raspberry Pi provided another unexpected advantage by allowing \textbf{remote programming and monitoring} through Ethernet and Wi-Fi. This meant that multiple developers could work simultaneously on the same unit, with no physical connection or wiring requirements. This also significantly simplified the \ac{EGSE} required for testing. However, as with any \acs{RF} system, care had to be taken to disable transmissions inside resonating structures (e.g.~\acs{TVAC} chambers) and before launch.

While RabbitMQ was a flexible databroker that allowed the effective use of Remote Process Calls and broadcast messages between microservices, it introduced a measurable performance overhead, including boot time, memory consumption, and latency.

The project suffered from the peak of the electronic component shortage in 2022. One of the shortcomings of the design was the inertia in making considered changes to the design versus performing patches on \acsp{PCB} and waiting for components to be available. Two components were mostly affected by this issue: Namely, the Arduino ATMega328 processor used as the dummy thermal generator, and an I2C multiplexer \ac{IC}. Both components were replaceable with a \acs{PCB} change: The Arduino processor with a similar stocked \acs{MCU}, and the multiplexer with an equivalent chip of a different form factor. Alternatively, earlier preparation would have called for an early stock of components during the \acs{PCB} design process.

The component shortage also proved difficult to navigate due to institutional procurement restrictions, combined with the fact that many stock items were only available from one or two days from standard distributors before being depleted.
